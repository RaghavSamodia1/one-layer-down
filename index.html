Europe

Europe Moved Up. Almost Nobody Profited.

The D and E segments absorbed all of Europe's 2025 growth while every other part of the market shrank. The four groups that own those segments earned mainstream margins. Chinese entrants took most of the increase. Stellantis sold 964 cars above the D-segment and Renault sold none.

The European car market grew by 53,916 units in 2025. The D and E segments grew by 192,798.

Every other part of the market contracted. D and E rose from 18.0% of EU registrations to 19.7% in a single year — D growing 10.4%, E growing 8.5%.

That is the entire net growth of the European car market, concentrated in the segments where European manufacturers are least evenly represented. What follows is what happened to the companies on either side of that line.

The shift is visible inside a single manufacturer

BMW is the clearest illustration, because the movement happened within one range in one year.

The 3 Series was BMW's best-selling European model in 2024 and its fourth in 2025. The two lines cross almost exactly — a loss of 14,046 against a gain of 14,320. Both figures reconcile against German registration data to within 1%, so the crossover is not a classification artefact.

The same shape runs through the rest of the brand: BMW's D-segment volume fell 6.5%, its E-segment volume rose 9.8%, and its F-segment fell 15.1%. The market pattern, reproduced inside one manufacturer's line-up.

One complication worth keeping. BMW's sub-D models — the 1 and 2 Series, X1 and X2 — grew 10%, in a band that contracted 1.6% market-wide. The brand gained share at both ends of its range and lost it only in the middle. That is a more awkward result than a clean premiumisation story, and it is the honest one.

The top of the market moved the other way

The F-segment fell 12.1%, and the detail matters because it determines where the opportunity is not.

Within saloons the split is almost perfectly by powertrain. Electric flagships collapsed — Tesla Model S −70%, Model X −64%, Audi e-tron GT −42%, Mercedes EQS −36% — while combustion models in the same price band grew: Lamborghini Urus +239%, Revuelto +96%, Bentley Continental GT +33%, Rolls-Royce Cullinan +23%, Mercedes G-Class +16%, Porsche Panamera +5%.

Luxury demand did not fall uniformly. Electric luxury demand fell, and the mechanical end of the market grew. Above roughly €100,000 the buyer is purchasing the thing that cannot be procured, which is precisely what an electric powertrain removes.

The prize is therefore the E-segment and the top of D, not the segment above them.

The companies that own those segments earned ordinary margins

Here a disclosure limit has to be stated before the numbers, because most writing on this subject ignores it: none of these companies report margin by segment, and none report margin by region. BMW's automotive EBIT margin covers every car it sells everywhere; Mercedes' return on sales includes Chinese discounting and US tariffs. Neither tells you what a 5-Series earns in Germany.

So the claim here is narrower than "the D-segment stopped paying". It is that the four groups whose business is overwhelmingly D-and-above have converged on margins indistinguishable from mass-market carmakers, and that the convergence is steepest at the top.

Three readings, in ascending order of how much work they do.

Tariffs are the shallow one. US import duties hit all four. BMW quantifies the effect at roughly 1.25 percentage points; Mercedes' own disclosure implies about 1.1, since it reports 5.0% including tariffs and 6.1% excluding them. Real, external, and not evidence of anything structural.

One-offs are the middle one. Porsche's 0.3% is not a trading result — it includes a €3.9bn writedown from reversing its electrification strategy. Strip it out and automotive EBITDA margin was 13.3% against 22.7% the year before. Anyone quoting 0.3% as operating performance is misreading the accounts; the honest version is 22.7% to 13.3%, plus a separate admission that a strategic bet failed.

What survives both adjustments is the point. Take out tariffs and the writedown: BMW is at roughly 6.5%, Mercedes 6.1%, Audi in the 4–6% band it guided to after cutting guidance twice, Porsche 13.3% and falling. Those are not the numbers of an industry sitting on a profit pool.

A note on what cannot be done with this data. Dividing group revenue by group units to derive an average selling price is the most common error in this analysis and it produces nothing: BMW's €133.5bn includes motorcycles and financial services, Mercedes' €132.2bn includes vans and mobility, and Porsche's automotive revenue is separable where the others' are not. Four different definitions, and the spread between them would look like a finding.

Mix engineering bought Mercedes nothing

BMW and Mercedes finished 2025 with revenue within one percent of each other — €133.5bn against €132.2bn — and automotive margins within 0.3 points. On the two measures that matter, they are the same company. They arrived by opposite routes.

Mercedes spent four years on an explicit up-market strategy: compressing the Entry tier, concentrating investment in Top-End, accepting volume loss for mix. In 2025 Top-End was 268,026 units, 15% of sales, down 5%. The S-Class — the clearest expression of the whole thesis — fell 17% to 105,564. Core fell 10%. Entry fell 10%. The tier the strategy was built to grow shrank, and only marginally less than the tiers it was built to de-emphasise.

BMW made no equivalent bet, kept breadth, and delivered 2,463,681 vehicles — roughly 260,000 more — for the same revenue and a marginally better margin.

Selling 260,000 more cars was worth as much as four years of mix engineering. That is falsifiable: if Mercedes' 2026 or 2027 results show Top-End growing while margin separates from BMW's, the strategy was early rather than wrong.

Chinese manufacturers added 141,463 units of D-and-above volume in twelve months, against a segment complex that grew by roughly 193,000 in total. The entrant group accounts for the majority of the increase.

That is the fuller answer to why a growing segment produced falling margins. The volume arrived; a large share of it went to manufacturers who were not there the year before; and the incumbents held price against them.

Against segment growth of 9.9%, Stellantis went backwards and Renault grew at less than half the rate. Both lost share in the only expanding part of the market.

Two of Europe's three largest makers are not in it at all

Above the D-segment specifically, Stellantis — fourteen brands — sold 964 cars in 2025: a Jeep Grand Cherokee, a Maserati GranTurismo, an MC20. Renault Group sold none; its largest vehicle of any kind is the Rafale, a D-segment SUV. Three German groups took 84% of E and F volume.

The standing objection is that only the German premium marques can charge E-segment money. The Volkswagen ID.7 sold 66,766 units in Europe in 2025 — more than the Mercedes E-Class at 42,483, behind only the 5-Series — and Volkswagen's entry model is a Polo. A mainstream European badge can hold the segment at €50–60,000. The question is not whether it is possible.

Stellantis has moved, to Wuhan. In May 2026 it signed a second joint venture with Dongfeng: two Peugeot electric models from 2027 based on the Concept 6 large saloon and Concept 8 large SUV, plus two Jeeps, in a roughly €1 billion programme of which Stellantis funds €130 million. That is the only route to the volume base the segment requires — a European-only programme amortises against 504,623 registrations shared across some forty nameplates — and at 13% of the cost it is an option rather than a bet.

The open question is destination. The announcement describes the output as intended for "the Chinese market and export to global markets"; Europe is not named. A Wuhan-built Peugeot BEV entering the EU faces countervailing duties applied by origin rather than badge, which already caught the Shanghai Model 3, the Dacia Spring and BMW's Chinese-built iX3. Watch for European type approval, not press releases.

Renault cannot copy it. No Chinese joint venture since 2020, no US presence, no volume base outside Europe. A clean-sheet large Renault funded on European volume alone is the option guaranteed to fail, and it has been tried twice — the Safrane and the Vel Satis.

Two routes remain. Rent the architecture: Geely holds 34% of Renault Korea, 26.4% of the Brazilian operation and roughly 45% of Horse Powertrain, and through Volvo, Polestar and Zeekr owns large-vehicle platforms Renault did not fund. Busan already builds restyled Geely product and is the one plant in the group with a large-vehicle line running. Or concede the segment and fund the C-to-D position, which is genuinely strong — Austral 57,181, Scénic 30,420, Rafale 22,264 — with the 4.2% growth rate understood as the cost.

Alpine is the unspent asset: the only badge in the group with headroom above the mainstream, currently applied to a sports car and a D-segment SUV.

Indicators

Whether Mercedes' Top-End tier grows in 2026. One down year is a market; two is a verdict on the mix strategy.

Whether the Wuhan Peugeots are type-approved for Europe. Decisive, answerable before 2027, and visible in filings rather than announcements.

Where the Concept 6 production model is priced. Below €60,000 it competes where the ID.7 has shown a mainstream badge works. Above €75,000 it is aimed at the band that fell 20.7%.

Whether Chinese D-and-above growth holds near 256%. At that rate the entrant group passes Stellantis's entire large-car business inside two years.

Whether Renault's answer arrives as a supply agreement rather than a programme. An architecture licence announced ahead of any product reveal is the asset-level route working.

Whether the ID.7 holds above 66,000. Much of the argument that this segment is enterable without a premium badge rests on it. A launch-year peak and a run rate look identical in one year of data.

We have seen this film

The Japanese entry into Western markets in the 1970s and 1980s produced exactly this reaction, and exactly the same category of response.

Western manufacturers did not, in the main, respond by getting better. They responded structurally: voluntary export restraints, and a generation of joint ventures designed to obtain by partnership what could not be obtained by engineering.

Every one of them is gone. Not one produced a durable structural advantage for the Western partner. NUMMI is the most instructive: it ran for twenty-six years, GM learned the Toyota Production System there, and GM still went bankrupt in 2009. The knowledge transferred; the outcome did not follow.

What actually happened is that Japanese manufacturers built local plants, Western manufacturers eventually improved their product, and the market reached an equilibrium in which both exist. The invasion narrative was correct about the entry and wrong about the outcome.

The current defensive structures deserve the same scepticism. Stellantis at 13% of a Dongfeng programme, Leapmotor distributed through Stellantis dealers, the various technology licensing arrangements — these are the joint ventures of the 1980s in contemporary dress, and the historical hit rate on them is close to zero.

Where the difference actually is

Two honest disanalogies, because the parallel is not perfect and pretending otherwise weakens it.

The Japanese never took the Western manufacturers' home market. Chinese brands did. GM's Chinese volume fell from 4.04m to just over 1.8m; Chevrolet's from 641,320 to 52,774. Western manufacturers were never significant in Japan, so there was nothing to lose there. This time the incumbents lost the largest market in the world before the competition arrived in theirs.

And the growth rate is not comparable to the level. Chinese brands at 4.3% is small. But they added 230,976 units in a year, in a market that grew 53,916 in total. The share is a foothold; the trajectory is not. Anyone arguing this is overblown has to hold both of those facts at once, and the honest version of the argument is not yet a crisis rather than not a threat.

What the companies handling it well have in common

They are not doing anything structurally clever. They are doing the thing they were already good at, harder.

Toyota resisted the pure-electric consensus and kept building hybrids while being told it was behind. The 2025 European data settled that argument: electric flagships fell 36–70% while combustion models in the same price band grew, and the F-segment decline was almost entirely a powertrain story. Toyota was not slow. It was right about what customers would buy.

BMW has €45.5bn, has bought nothing in twenty-five years, and is answering the Neue Klasse question with its own engineering. It grew 4.1% in Europe in 2025 against a market that grew 0.5%, and it did it while retaining the six-cylinder engines and rear-drive layouts its customers came for.

Renault kept its European core entirely unpartnered — the plants, Ampere, the small-EV architecture — while partnering on peripheral geographies and the combustion business it is exiting. Its European volume grew 6.1%. The R5 is a retro small car built in France, which is the least technologically radical product strategy available and it is working.

Suzuki never chased any of it, stayed in the segments and markets it understands, and remains one of the most consistently profitable volume manufacturers in the industry.

The common factor is not defensiveness. It is that all four declined to reorganise themselves around a competitor's strategy, and instead did more of what their customers already valued them for.

What to take from it

Do not restructure around the entrant. The 1980s joint ventures were all rational at the time and all dissolved. Structure does not substitute for product.

Do not concede the segments you are good at in order to chase the ones the entrant defined. The manufacturers losing share fastest in Europe are not the ones that ignored Chinese competition; they are the ones that were already weak in their own core.

Do learn the specific things worth learning. Cost engineering, development speed, and supplier integration are genuine Chinese advantages and can be adopted without adopting the strategy. Toyota's Chinese development centre and Honda's Momenta and CATL arrangements are exactly this — importing capability, not restructuring around a rival.

And keep the counter in view. 4.3% with a doubling growth rate is a different problem in three years. The argument here is that the response should be product rather than structure, not that there is nothing to respond to.

Indicators

Whether Chinese share passes 8% of the EU market. At that point the level joins the trajectory and this argument needs revisiting.

Whether the current wave of joint ventures outlasts the 1980s ones. NUMMI ran twenty-six years and produced nothing durable for its Western partner. The Stellantis–Leapmotor and Stellantis–Dongfeng arrangements are the test cases.

Whether MG keeps growing. It is 46% of the Chinese position and an eighteen-year-old business. If the newer entrants overtake it, the composition of the threat changes materially.

Whether the F-segment powertrain split repeats in 2026. One year is a market; two is a preference.

The entrants

China's EV Transition Claims Its Biggest Name Yet

Chevrolet sold 641,320 cars in China in 2018 and 52,774 in 2024. It is the largest brand ever withdrawn from the Chinese market — larger than Suzuki and Mitsubishi combined — and the first of GM's three Chinese brands to go, roughly a century after the company's first Shanghai dealership.

On 4 August 2026 General Motors extended its joint venture with SAIC Motor by twenty years, to 2047, and in the same announcement confirmed it will discontinue Chevrolet sales in China. Production continues there, through the separate SAIC-GM-Wuling venture, for export.

The brand being withdrawn is the one GM started with.

A century in China, and how it was built

GM first reached China in the 1920s through a Chevrolet dealership in Shanghai. That business ended in 1949. Negotiations to return began in 1993, and Shanghai GM — a 50:50 joint venture with Shanghai Automotive Industry Corporation — was formed in June 1997.

Buick, not Chevrolet, was the brand GM returned with. The first vehicle off the Shanghai line was a Buick Regal in April 1999, and the reception was such that within a few years the large majority of all Buicks built anywhere were sold in China. A marque that had become an also-ran in the United States was rebuilt as a Chinese premium brand.

The scale that followed was without precedent for a foreign manufacturer. In 2010 Shanghai GM became the first passenger-car maker in China to sell a million vehicles in a single year. GM led all global automakers in the Chinese market for six consecutive years and peaked at 4.04 million units in 2017 — more vehicles than it sold in the United States.

The joint venture that has just been extended to 2047 was signed in 1997. If it runs its term it will be a fifty-year partnership.

The volume that remains is concentrated in SAIC-GM-Wuling, the budget venture, rather than in the three GM brands. The Chinese business had already become a low-cost manufacturing partnership more than a branded consumer business; the new agreement formalises that.

What GM has decided

The two halves of the announcement look contradictory and are not.

China is no longer a market for the volume brand. Chevrolet retail ends. Buick and Cadillac continue, in a market where GM holds 7.1% and where its products have not been competitive on electric powertrains.

China remains a factory, for twenty more years. Chevrolet output continues for export. Buick and Cadillac production is directed at the Middle East, Africa, South America, Mexico and other Asian markets. SAIC-GM has committed to at least 30 electric and hybrid models by 2030.

Eight consecutive years of decline is a settled result rather than a slump, so conceding the Chinese consumer was not really a choice. Retaining the cost base is the choice — and on what Chinese plants, supply chains and battery components now cost, it is the defensible one.

What follows

The competition that removed Chevrolet has not stopped. Buick and Cadillac face the same domestic brands and the same electric deficit, and both are already down 67% and 45% from their peaks. The question is not whether Chevrolet was worth withdrawing at 52,774 units, but how long the two GM kept have on the same trajectory.

GM's export platform is also its export competitor. SAIC is one of China's largest vehicle exporters in its own right, principally through MG. GM will now ship Buicks and Cadillacs from plants co-owned by a company shipping its own brands into several of the same destinations.

The destination list is the most informative line in the announcement. The Middle East, Africa, South America, Mexico and other parts of Asia are collectively the major markets that have not imposed origin-based duties on Chinese-built vehicles. The European Union has, and the United States has. Neither is named.

Twenty years is a long commitment to a relationship two governments are actively unwinding. Whatever the logic of keeping the plants, the political exposure is not priced in anything disclosed.

Indicators

Whether Buick and Cadillac stabilise in 2026. They are now the whole of GM's branded Chinese retail, and the precedent for what happens when they do not is 52,774 units.

Whether the next foreign withdrawal is larger than Chevrolet's. Volkswagen, Toyota, Honda and Nissan all retain far bigger Chinese businesses than Chevrolet had. If any announces a brand-level retreat, the ceiling on this pattern has not been found.

Whether GM discloses China-built export volumes separately. The export arrangement is the substance of the deal and is currently unmeasurable from outside.

Whether the 30-model electric and hybrid programme survives. It is a large commitment in a market where the partnership has lost share for most of a decade.

Where the growth is manufactured

The electric Aceman is built at Zhangjiagang, in Jiangsu province, by Spotlight Automotive — a 50:50 joint venture between BMW Group and Great Wall Motor. So is the electric Cooper.

In October 2024 the European Union imposed a 20.7% countervailing duty on vehicles from that joint venture, following its anti-subsidy investigation into Chinese-built electric cars. The European production route has not materialised

In September 2023 BMW announced that Oxford would begin assembling electric MINIs, including the Aceman, from 2026. That plan has been postponed, the group citing "multiple uncertainties". No replacement date for European assembly of the electric range has been confirmed.

India

In China They Brought the Technology. In India They Are Buying It.

Nikkei reports that global carmakers are replicating their China strategy to gain a foothold in India. The two deals cited run the other way round. In China the foreign manufacturer supplied the engineering and held half the equity; in India it is purchasing the engineering and giving up the equity. That is not the same template — and the template being invoked ended with the foreign partners leaving.

On 12 August 2026, Nikkei Asia reported that global carmakers are turning to Indian partners to establish themselves in India, borrowing from the approach they used in China, with the tie-ups "increasingly crucial to managing capital expenditure." The example given is Honda, which has engaged a Tata Group company on platform development.

The observation that partnerships are multiplying is correct. The comparison to China does not survive contact with what either arrangement actually contains.

What the China strategy was

From 1994, a foreign manufacturer could not build passenger cars in China except through a joint venture in which it held no more than 50%. The requirement was lifted in stages — electric vehicles from the end of 2018, commercial vehicles from 2020, passenger cars from 1 January 2022.

Two features defined it, and both are load-bearing.

It was compulsory. No foreign manufacturer chose a 50:50 structure in China. It was the statutory price of the market.

The foreign partner supplied the technology. The local partner supplied the licence, the land, the distribution and the political standing. The foreign partner supplied the platform, the powertrain, the manufacturing method and the badge. That division is why the knowledge-transfer literature exists at all.

Neither feature applies to India. There has never been a local-ownership requirement for carmakers, which is why Hyundai entered wholly owned in 1996, Kia wholly owned in 2019, and General Motors and Ford operated wholly-owned Indian subsidiaries until they left.

So whatever is happening in India, it is not the thing that happened in China. In India it is voluntary, and when a structure is voluntary the direction of the transaction tells you what is actually scarce.

Honda is buying engineering. Volkswagen had already cut its planned Indian EV platform investment from around $1bn to roughly $700m in November 2025, and had failed to reach a viable programme with Mahindra before that. Its Indian electric cars are now to run on an Indianised version of CMP21, an architecture developed by Volkswagen's Chinese operation in 24 months and reported at 30–40% below the cost of MEB.

That last detail is the only genuine China connection in the story, and it is the opposite of a joint venture. Volkswagen is importing engineering IP that its Chinese business created, into a country where it is simultaneously reducing its own shareholding. Nothing about that resembles Shanghai 1997.

What is actually being replicated

Something from the Chinese experience is repeating in India. It is in the third and fourth rows of that table, not in the ownership column.

The Indian version is much earlier and much gentler — Maruti at 39% is not Chevrolet at 52,774 units, and Toyota grew 18.6% and Kia 13.3%, so foreign is not a category that is losing. But the mechanism is the same one, and it is the part of the China story that a partnership does not address. Selling 51% of your Indian subsidiary to a steel conglomerate does not make the product more competitive against a Scorpio-N.

What the Honda deal actually is

The Honda–Tata Technologies arrangement is the more interesting of the two and is being filed under the wrong heading.

It is not about gaining a foothold in India. The platform is for Asia and other global markets and explicitly excludes North America. Honda sells around 7,500 cars a month in India. The deal is an engineering services contract, placed in India because Indian engineering is capable and cheap, for products largely sold elsewhere.

That is the same movement as Volkswagen having its Chinese operation develop CMP21 and then shipping the architecture to India, and the same movement as Ford restarting its Chennai plant in 2026 to build roughly 235,000 engines a year purely for export. Development and componentry are relocating to wherever they are cheapest to produce, decoupled from where the vehicles are sold. India is winning that on cost and engineering depth.

Calling it a China-style joint venture obscures what is worth noticing: Honda, a company that has never let an outsider design its platforms, has concluded it cannot afford to design them itself. The capital-expenditure pressure in the Nikkei piece is real. The instrument being reached for is not a joint venture; it is outsourcing.

Whether Honda extends the Tata Technologies relationship beyond one platform. One programme is cost control. A second is a decision about where Honda develops cars.

Whether Maruti Suzuki's share stabilises above 35%. The domestic-share argument turns on whether the FY26 move was a step or a trend.

That explanation is also the one supported by this body of work elsewhere, which is a reason to hold the argument here loosely.

India Taxes Petrol Cars by Size. It Taxes Electric Cars Flat.

The September 2025 GST reform put small petrol cars on 18% and large ones on 40%, and left every electric car on 5% regardless of size. The size test was not extended to electric vehicles, so the incentive to go electric is worth roughly twenty times more on a ₹40 lakh SUV than on a ₹5 lakh hatchback. The one scheme that does condition on price sets a floor, not a ceiling.

India's goods and services tax on cars was restructured with effect from 22 September 2025. Two slabs replaced the old rate-plus-cess structure:

The first two rows are a deliberate size-based progressivity that India has run in some form since the 1980s: small cars are taxed lightly because most buyers can only afford small cars. The third row is a decarbonisation incentive. Each is defensible on its own.

The interaction is not, and it appears to be nobody's decision.

The arithmetic

The electric discount is the gap between what a car would pay on petrol and the flat 5%. That gap is 13 points for a small car and 35 points for a large one — and it applies to a much larger base.

The same policy hands a ₹65,000 inducement to the buyer of a hatchback and a ₹14,00,000 inducement to the buyer of a large SUV. That is a ratio of about 21.5 to 1, and it is entirely a consequence of applying a flat rate on one side of a progressive schedule.

Put the other way: India decided that a petrol car longer than four metres should pay more than twice the tax of one shorter than four metres, and then decided that an electric car should pay 5% whether it is three metres long or five and a half.

The other two levers point the same way

The GST differential is now the main instrument, because the direct one was withdrawn.

Purchase subsidies for electric cars no longer exist. FAME-II ended, and PM E-DRIVE — ₹10,900 crore, covering roughly 25 lakh two-wheelers, 3 lakh three-wheelers and 14,000 buses — excludes electric four-wheelers entirely. The stated reasoning was that the concessional GST rate is sufficient support. That is the rate whose benefit scales with price.

And the import scheme has a price floor. Under SPMEPCI, an approved manufacturer may import up to 8,000 electric cars a year at 15% duty instead of the standing 70–100%, in exchange for ₹4,150 crore of investment and 50% domestic value addition within five years. The vehicles must have a minimum CIF value of US$35,000.

That is the only place in the structure where policy conditions explicitly on price, and it conditions upward. A manufacturer wanting to import an affordable electric car receives nothing; the concession exists only above roughly ₹31 lakh landed, which is a retail price well over ₹40 lakh.

So: no subsidy for cars, a tax break that grows with price, and an import concession available only above a price floor. Three instruments, all tilted the same way, none of which was designed to be.

This is the fact that cuts against the argument, and it should come before the argument rather than after it.

Two things follow, and they are not in tension.

The regressive structure has not distorted the volume mix, so far. The bottom of the market is where Indian car demand is, and a ₹65,000 tax advantage on a ₹6 lakh car is a large proportion of that car's price even though it is a small number. Proportionally, the incentive is not regressive at all — it is only regressive in rupees per vehicle.

But rupees per vehicle is what a fiscal incentive costs, and what a buyer at the top receives. BMW's Indian electric volume grew 123.9% to 3,537 units. On the arithmetic above, each of those cars carries a tax concession in the seven figures. The foregone revenue per unit at the top of the market is roughly twenty times that at the bottom, and nothing in the design was intended to produce that.

Why it is likely to matter more from here

The mix is what changes. Mahindra's electric volume rose more than fivefold in FY26 on the BE 6 and XEV 9e, which sit in the ₹18–30 lakh band rather than the ₹10 lakh one. Every manufacturer's electric roadmap moves up-market, because that is where the battery cost is easiest to absorb — and the tax structure rewards exactly that movement.

An incentive that is proportionally neutral and absolutely regressive becomes a fiscal problem when the absolutely-regressive end starts carrying the volume. On current product plans it will.

What a corrected version would look like

Stated as options, not a recommendation: the structure only needs the size test extended across the powertrain boundary.

Apply the existing 4,000mm and ground-clearance tests to electric cars. Small EVs stay at 5%; large EVs move to a second, still-concessional rate — 12% or 18% — preserving the electric advantage everywhere while capping it in rupees. This requires no new definition, only the application of one that already exists.

Or cap the concession in absolute terms — 5% up to a stated value, standard rate above it. This is the more common instrument internationally and the harder one to administer in a market where ex-showroom prices vary by state.

Or leave it, and accept the cost as the price of speed. A defensible position while penetration is 4.3% and the objective is adoption at any price point. It becomes indefensible at 15%.

Indicators

Whether any GST Council paper addresses the electric-vehicle size question. The reform of September 2025 restructured everything else and left this untouched, which suggests it has not been raised rather than that it was considered and rejected.

Whether SPMEPCI's $35,000 floor is revised. It exists to prevent the concession being used for cheap imports, which is a coherent aim; but it is also the clearest statement in Indian policy that the electric benefit is aimed above the mass market.

Whether the ₹20 lakh-plus band's share of electric volume rises materially in FY27. That is the threshold at which the rupee cost of the flat rate becomes visible in revenue terms.

The disconfirming signal. This piece argues the flat rate creates a large and unintended up-market tilt. If electric volume stays concentrated below ₹15 lakh as penetration rises through 10%, the tilt is theoretical — the structure would be generous at the top and simply not used there, which is a much smaller problem than the arithmetic suggests.

The Renault–Nissan alliance

Renault Ended the Alliance and Kept Europe

Part one of four. The equity partnership with Nissan is being dismantled on a published schedule. The replacement arrangements with Geely are structured on entirely different principles — and not one of them touches the European vehicle business, which Renault has funded and kept alone. That is the decision the rest of this series follows from.

Renault is usually described as a company recovering from its Nissan dependency, or as one that has swapped a Japanese partner for a Chinese one. Both readings miss what the documents actually show.

Renault dismantled an equity alliance, built a set of asset-level partnerships in its place, and drew a line around the part of the business it would not partner on at all. The line is the interesting part, and it is where this piece ends.

The replacement is a different instrument

The Nissan alliance was cross-shareholdings between parent companies — an attempt to align two whole firms through equity, which produced two decades of governance friction and one criminal case. The Geely arrangements have no parent-level equity at all. They are stakes in assets:

Geely stopped buying carmakers in 2019

The instrument is not specific to Renault, and its history explains the change.

Geely's acquisition era ran 2006 to 2019: Volvo Cars from Ford for $1.8 billion in 2010, a 9.7% Daimler stake in 2018, Proton and Lotus in 2017. As a portfolio it bought engineering capability, brand credibility and market access. As a balance sheet it also bought pension liabilities, legacy plants, union agreements and an obligation to keep marginal businesses alive.

Since 2022 the pattern has been consistent: a minority stake in a specific asset, product brought to fill its unused capacity, ownership and liabilities left with the incumbent. Renault Korea, Renault do Brasil and Horse all fit. So does the Ford joint venture at Almussafes announced in July 2026 — 34% for around €221 million, valuing the plant at roughly €650 million, giving Geely its first European manufacturing base without a brand changing hands.

Sixteen years ago Geely bought Volvo Cars from Ford for $1.8 billion. In 2026 it took a third of Ford's Spanish factory for €221 million. Same counterparty, one-eighth the price, comparable European industrial access. What changed is the definition of the valuable asset: in 2010 it was a brand, in 2026 a building with the right postcode.

An estimate, and what it is worth

Everything in this section is the author's own modelling. It is not reported, not disclosed, and should be weighted well below the figures above.

Renault's supply arrangements with Nissan and Mitsubishi generate revenue that is not separately disclosed. A first attempt to size it produced roughly €1.5bn for 2025 and €2.5bn for 2030, built from visible headline programmes. That understated it, because it omitted three asset classes:

  1. Nissan's European light commercial twins — Interstar, Primastar, Townstar. Individually unglamorous enough to be forgotten in a top-down estimate; collectively substantial and stable, because LCV cycles are long.
  2. The Micra at Douai — a full passenger programme treated as a badging exercise rather than a revenue line.
  3. Mitsubishi's European platform twins — Colt, ASX and Grandis. An entire brand's European presence supplied from Renault architecture, sitting outside the Nissan relationship and therefore outside the mental model.

Rebuilt from enumerated assets, the estimates move to roughly €3bn (2025) and €5.5bn (2030).

The general lesson is more reliable than the numbers: revenue projections built top-down from strategic narrative systematically understate, because narrative remembers flagship programmes and forgets vans. Build from enumerated assets, plant by plant and nameplate by nameplate, or do not build.

A crossover date follows from this model — the point where Renault's Geely-linked business exceeds its Nissan-linked one, around 2028–29. Earlier drafts of this analysis led with that date. It should not lead, because it rests entirely on the estimates above rather than on anything disclosed, and a single unreported assumption moves it by years. It is a directional statement about which relationship is growing, not a forecast.

What Renault becomes

Judgement, from the reported material above.

Renault owns plants — Chennai, Douai, and parts of Busan and Paraná. It owns architectures — CMF-B, CMF-C/D, AmpR Small, and through Horse a share of a powertrain portfolio. It increasingly monetises them by supplying other people's brands, while renting in capabilities it chose not to fund.

Two consequences, pointing opposite ways.

Favourably: supply revenue is structurally less cyclical and less capital-hungry at the margin than brand revenue. Renault is converting fixed assets from a cost of competing into a source of income, which is the right move for a mid-scale manufacturer that cannot win a global volume war.

Unfavourably: a supplier does not control its customers' roadmaps, and one competing with its own customers is in an unstable position. Renault sells Nissan the architecture for the Micra and competes with the Micra. It builds Geely product in Busan and competes with Geely in Europe.

And the cost of the line it drew round Europe is the subject of part three: a company that funds Europe alone has only European scale to amortise against, which is why it has nothing above the D-segment.

Indicators

The 10% floor. Both sides hold the option to reduce from 15%. Whoever exercises first, and who buys, says more than any joint statement.

Whether Busan or Paraná receives a second Geely product. One restyled model is a capacity deal; two is a platform commitment.

Whether Nissan sources from Renault outside Europe and India. Confined to those regions this is a legacy wind-down being monetised. Extended to ASEAN or South America, Renault has built a merchant business.

Whether a fifth Geely asset deal follows the template. Four is a pattern; five with a new counterparty in a new region makes it the standard instrument.

The disconfirming signal. This piece argues Renault deliberately ring-fenced its European core. If Renault takes a partner into a European vehicle plant, into Ampere, or into its next passenger architecture, that reading is wrong — and the more ordinary explanation, that it partners wherever capital is short, would be right instead.

The Renault–Nissan alliance

The Divorced Children: What Remains, and the Stakes

Part two of four. Nothing that survives the lengthy breakup is jointly owned. What is left is a set of separately negotiated supply contracts, each priced on its own, between three companies that still hold each other's shares out of habit.

Part one described the equity alliance as terminal. That raises an obvious question, because Renault, Nissan and Mitsubishi visibly still work together: Nissans are built in Renault factories, Mitsubishis are rebadged Renaults, and the Indian operation runs on shared plant.

The answer is that all of it is now transactional. Every surviving link is a supply agreement with its own terms, its own duration and its own price. Not one of them is a shared asset, a joint programme, or a pooled investment. That distinction is what separates an alliance from a customer relationship, and it is the whole content of what remains.

What the residual equity buys, operationally, is nothing. It confers no board control, no product veto, no joint planning obligation. It is a financial position in a company you also trade with, and the two facts are no longer connected.

Europe: three supply contracts, priced separately

Nissan's light commercial range is Renault's. The Townstar, Primastar and Interstar are the Kangoo, Trafic and Master. In the 2025 EU registration data they total 9,182 units against Nissan's 193,407 — under 5% of the European business, because Nissan's European volume is overwhelmingly the Sunderland-built Qashqai and Juke.

Nissan's next small cars are Renault's. The new Micra is built by Ampere at Douai on Renault's small-EV architecture. A Twingo derivative follows — designed by Nissan, engineered and produced by Renault. This is the boundary moving: light commercials were peripheral, small passenger cars are not.

Mitsubishi's European range is largely Renault's already. The Colt is a Clio, the ASX a Captur, the Grandis a Symbioz. In 2025 those three were 59% of Mitsubishi's 43,356 EU registrations, up from 46% a year earlier — not because Mitsubishi bought more, but because its own product fell 43% while the borrowed product held.

Three arrangements, three different depths, and no common structure. Nissan buys vans and is starting to buy small cars. Mitsubishi buys most of a range. Each was negotiated on its own terms.

India: the cleanest case, because the ownership actually moved

India is where the transactional logic is most explicit, because an asset changed hands rather than being shared.

Renault acquired Nissan's 51% of Renault Nissan Automotive India Private Limited and took full ownership of the Chennai plant from August 2025 — a site with over 400,000 units of capacity that has built more than 2.8 million vehicles since 2010.

What Nissan kept: roughly 300 dealer outlets, forty years of Indian brand equity, an export programme, and a capacity reservation at a factory it no longer owns.

What Nissan gave up: any say in what the factory builds.

The evidence that this is settled rather than transitional is the product. Nissan's locally built Indian range is the Gravite — a Renault Triber — and the Tekton, a Duster on CMF-B. Every Nissan built in India is a Renault underneath.

That is not a partnership dissolving messily. It is a partnership that has finished dissolving and been replaced by a purchase order.

What each party is actually holding

Renault holds the assets. Chennai outright. Douai, and the small-EV architecture the Micra and Twingo derivatives run on. CMF-B. The European light-commercial range that Nissan sells under its own badge. Renault emerged from the breakup owning the industrial layer the other two now buy from.

Nissan holds a brand and a network, and one genuine manufacturing base. Sunderland, the Qashqai and the Juke are Nissan's own and account for 77% of its European volume. In India it holds distribution without production. The position is coherent where it owns product and thin where it does not.

Mitsubishi holds the least. A 42,000-unit European business that is majority someone else's product and falling, with its own range down 43% in a year. Its real business is elsewhere — ASEAN at 256,000 units of its 797,000 global total — and the European arrangement is a small operation kept alive by supply rather than a strategy.

Why "alliance" is now the wrong word

An alliance implies pooled risk. These arrangements pool nothing.

If the Micra fails at Douai, Renault carries the plant and Nissan carries the marketing. If Mitsubishi's Colt stops selling, Renault loses a customer and Mitsubishi loses a model. If Chennai runs below capacity, that is Renault's problem alone — Nissan reserved capacity, it did not buy any.

Each party's downside is bounded by its own contract. That is a normal commercial relationship between manufacturers, and it is what the alliance has become: three companies that trade with each other, hold each other's shares for reasons that predate the current arrangement, and have no joint obligation beyond the specific agreements signed.

The lengthy breakup did not end the cooperation. It ended the shared exposure, which is the only part that made it an alliance.

What Next for Renault: Time to Go Big in Europe

The lengthy breakup left Renault owning the industrial layer: Chennai outright, Douai and the small-EV architecture, CMF-B, and a share of the powertrain business it extracted into Horse. Parts one and two set out how that happened and what it left the other two partners holding.

This part asks the harder question. Renault emerged from the breakup as the party with the assets. What should it do with them?

So the position is not that Renault is under-represented in a premium niche. It is absent from the only growing part of its home market while new entrants take it.

What Renault cannot do

It cannot build its way there. A clean-sheet large Renault would have to amortise against European volume alone: 504,623 E-segment registrations shared across roughly forty nameplates. Renault has no Chinese joint venture, having exited in 2020, no US presence, and no volume base outside Europe of consequence.

This has been attempted twice. The Safrane and the Vel Satis were both funded on European volume and neither reached scale. The arithmetic has not improved since — it has worsened, because the segment now contains Chinese entrants pricing against a domestic cost base.

And it should not aim at the top of the market. The F-segment fell 12.1% in 2025, with saloons down 20.7%. Within saloons the decline is almost perfectly a powertrain story: Tesla Model S −70%, Model X −64%, Audi e-tron GT −42%, Mercedes EQS −36%, while combustion models in the same band grew — Urus +239%, Continental GT +33%, Cullinan +23%, Panamera +5%. Above roughly €100,000 the buyer is purchasing something that cannot be procured, which is exactly what an electric powertrain removes. No volume manufacturer can compete there.

The target is the E-segment, which grew 8.5%, and the top of D, which grew 10.4%.

What the ID.7 proves

The standing objection is that only the German premium marques can charge E-segment money.

The Volkswagen ID.7 registered 66,766 units in Europe in 2025 — more than the Mercedes E-Class at 42,483, behind only the BMW 5-Series. Volkswagen's entry model in Europe is a Polo.

A mainstream European badge can hold the E-segment at €50–60,000. That is settled as a matter of fact, by a direct competitor, last year. The question for Renault is not whether it is possible.

What Renault can do

Rent the architecture. The counterparty is already under contract. Geely holds 34% of Renault Korea, 26.4% of the Brazilian operation and roughly 45% of Horse Powertrain, and through Volvo, Polestar and Zeekr it owns large-vehicle platforms — SPA and SEA — that Renault did not fund.

The specific architectures matter here, because Renault's own pipeline stops at the wrong size. Its funded platforms are CMF-B, CMF-C/D and AmpR Small — supermini to D-segment. It has nothing above that and no programme to build one.

Wishful thinking: the transaction nobody has proposed

What follows is speculation, clearly marked as such. It is not a forecast and nothing in the reporting suggests it is under discussion. It is included because the assets involved fit together unusually well, and because the alternative currently being pursued looks worse.

Three facts from this series sit adjacent to each other.

Renault holds dead equity. 15% of Nissan directly, plus a residual position in a French trust being sold down, with the required floor lowered to 10% in March 2025. It confers no control, no product access and no board influence. It is a financial holding in a company Renault also sells to.

Infiniti has retail capacity it cannot currently fill. Nissan's premium brand delivered 153,415 US vehicles in 2017 and 58,070 in 2024, pacing near 50,000 in 2025. Published dealer counts range from 191 to 342 and are unreliable, but the ratio is not: throughput per outlet is down roughly 67% whatever the denominator.

This is the fact that has moved against the argument, and it should be stated before the argument rather than after it. Nissan has committed to five new Infiniti models over five years — the QX65 in 2026, then a midsize hybrid SUV, a performance V6 sedan and two large hybrid SUVs. That is four additions on top of the three surviving nameplates, and it is a company refilling a network rather than abandoning one.

Alpine needs American retail and has none. It has a confirmed 2027 US entry, a seven-model plan, and a US-relevant European track record of 2,236 cars once the A290 is excluded.

The transaction: Renault exchanges part of its Nissan holding for a position in Infiniti — equity, a distribution agreement, or both — and enters North America through the Infiniti network.

What each party would get. Renault converts a stake that does nothing into US retail access it cannot otherwise buy at its scale, and gives Alpine a network on day one rather than building one for a two-thousand-unit brand. Nissan converts an underused premium network into a partner-funded one, reduces a cross-shareholding both sides have already agreed to shrink, and keeps Infiniti alive without funding a full premium programme alone. Infiniti dealers get product in a showroom running at a third of its scaled volume.

Why it will probably not happen, stated as plainly as the case for it:

  • Alpine is reportedly going elsewhere. It is described as in talks with AutoNation for an independent US retail presence rather than alliance channels. If that is right, Renault has already considered the alliance route and rejected it — which is itself the most interesting fact in this piece, because it means alliance membership no longer confers a retail advantage over what can be bought on the open market.
  • Brand adjacency cuts the wrong way. An Alpine counter inside a Nissan store that also houses Infiniti is three brands deep and premium in none of them.
  • Neither party wants deeper entanglement. The whole direction of the breakup has been toward arm's-length contracts. A joint retail venture reverses it.
  • Nissan is filling the showrooms itself. Five models in five years is capital committed. A network with product coming is not spare capacity, and the window in which this transaction made obvious sense may have closed while it was being described.
  • Alpine has already changed its US product twice — from mid-size and large electric crossovers to a next-generation A110 with a combustion option, reportedly because of tariffs and the reduction of US EV incentives. A company revising its product and powertrain is not in a position to also revise its distribution model.

The case for taking it seriously anyway is narrower than it was a year ago, and it is this: Renault's alternative is still to fund an American retail network from scratch for a brand selling roughly two thousand relevant cars a year, while holding a Nissan stake it has agreed to reduce and cannot otherwise deploy. A shared showroom with a brand that now has five models coming is a different proposition from a rescue of an empty one — arguably a better one for Alpine, since the traffic returns — but it is no longer a transaction where one side is obviously desperate. That was the version that would have been cheap.

Whether the AutoNation arrangement is confirmed or lapses. It is reported rather than announced. A signed agreement settles how Alpine reaches American buyers; a return to Nissan channels would indicate the alliance retains more operational value than the current direction suggests.

Whether either party moves below the 10% floor. The option exists on both sides. Whoever exercises it, and who buys, determines whether any equity-for-access transaction is even available.

Whether Renault's European large-car answer arrives as a supply agreement rather than a programme. An architecture licence — most plausibly Geely-sourced — announced ahead of any product reveal is the asset-level route working. An internally funded flagship is the Vel Satis for a third time.

Whether Alpine is given a product above €60,000. It is the only badge in the group that could carry one, and it is currently being spent on a hot hatch and a D-segment SUV.

Whether Infiniti's two announced electric models arrive and hold the brand above 50,000 US units. If they do, the network is committed and the speculation above is moot. If they do not, the question of what Infiniti's retail is for becomes live for Nissan whether or not Renault is interested.

Where the actual problems are

Nissan's difficulty is American and Chinese, not European. US sales fell 14.0% and Chinese sales 11.6% in FY2025, and the company posted an operating loss. Europe is 6% of the business and roughly stable. Any restructuring that begins with the European range is addressing the smallest available number.

Within North America the specific issue is Infiniti, which delivered 153,415 US vehicles in 2017 and 58,070 in 2024, pacing near 50,000 in 2025. Standalone showrooms are folding into Nissan stores, the QX50 and QX55 are discontinued, and throughput per outlet is down roughly two-thirds.

But Nissan is refilling it rather than winding it down, and the product plan is the clearest statement of intent the brand has made in a decade. Five new models in five years: the QX65, a coupe-profile midsize SUV arriving in 2026, followed by a midsize hybrid SUV, a performance V6 sedan and two large hybrid SUVs — four additions on top of the three nameplates that survive the discontinuations.

Two things about that plan are worth noting. It is a commitment of real capital to a brand many observers had written off, which changes what the network is for. And the powertrains have moved: earlier reporting had the renewal built around two electric models, and what has been described since is four hybrids and a combustion sedan. That is the same retreat from electric luxury visible in the European F-segment, where electric flagships fell 36–70% in 2025 while combustion models in the same price band grew.

And in India, Nissan owns no plant at all. It exited the RNAIPL joint venture; Renault took full ownership of Chennai from August 2025. Nissan retains roughly 300 dealer outlets, forty years of brand equity, an export programme and a capacity reservation at a factory it no longer owns. The structural weakness is worth naming: a company with one supplier is not a customer, it is a subsidiary with extra steps. Nissan's Indian product cadence, segment coverage and cost position are all set by a firm that competes with it in the same showrooms. If Renault does not build a midsize SUV, Nissan does not sell one.

Nissan management has said it is open to contract manufacturing or collaboration with other Indian manufacturers, with no restrictions on who it would consider, and has separately confirmed the Chennai capacity reservation. Read together, those statements say the Renault arrangement is non-exclusive and Nissan intends to keep it that way.

The obvious second supplier is Stellantis, and specifically Citroën. Stellantis holds three Indian manufacturing positions — Thiruvallur, the CK Birla joint venture building Citroën's C-Cubed range; Hosur, a powertrain plant positioned as a global ICE export hub; and Ranjangaon, the 50:50 Tata joint venture that has built over 1.37 million vehicles. Citroën's Indian retail volumes are a small fraction of what that footprint can absorb.

The asymmetry runs exactly opposite to the Renault one. Stellantis has Indian plants and no volume; Nissan has volume potential and no plants. Citroën's C3, Basalt and C3 Aircross sit on a Smart Car platform configured down to Indian price points — precisely the segment where Nissan's Indian range is thinnest and where Renault has nothing to lend it.

Both parties have done this before. Nissan has badge-engineered Renault product in India for a decade, from the Terrano to the Tekton and Gravite. Stellantis has supplied badge-engineered vehicles to brands it does not own — the Toyota ProAce, the Ram ProMaster City. Neither would be conceding anything it has not already conceded in public.

The tell, if it happens, is a capacity announcement at Thiruvallur or Hosur before any product announcement: utilisation moves first, badges follow. The disconfirming signal is equally clear — if Nissan's next Indian launch is a third Renault derivative, it has accepted single-supplier dependence as the price of capital discipline.

Mitsubishi's difficulty is that its strong region is its least defensible one. ASEAN at 256,000 units is the business, and it is the market Chinese manufacturers are entering hardest — BYD, GWM and others have been building capacity in Thailand and Indonesia specifically. Mitsubishi withdrew from China in 2023 after being outcompeted there; the same competitors are now arriving in the markets it retreated to.

North America at 165,000 units is its second region and depends on a small range in a market where its brand equity is thin.

What each should do

Mitsubishi should formalise Europe rather than fund it. At 42,000 units and 59% borrowed, the honest structures are a defined distribution agreement with fixed product and terms, or withdrawal as it withdrew from China. Continuing to fund a shrinking independent range alongside a growing borrowed one is the most expensive available option, and the least likely to produce a European business worth having.

The capital that releases belongs in ASEAN, where Mitsubishi has manufacturing, distribution and forty years of position, and where it is about to be attacked by the companies that removed it from China.

Nissan should resolve Infiniti rather than manage it. The brand is either worth refilling with product — which means committing to a premium programme in a segment it has been losing for eight years — or worth converting into something else. Part three of this series argues that the something else has an obvious counterparty in Alpine, which needs American retail and has none.

And Nissan should be explicit about which architectures it is buying and which it is keeping. Sunderland, the Qashqai and the Juke are real assets. The Micra and Twingo derivatives move the boundary of what Nissan designs. Renting specific architectures it declined to fund is defensible; letting the boundary move because each individual decision is cheaper than the alternative is how a company stops being one. Those two look identical for several years and then do not.

Maruti Suzuki and NEXA

NEXA Worked. That's Exactly Maruti's Problem.

How India's most successful retail experiment quietly locked its creator out of the market's most profitable decade — and why a second premium brand is no longer on the table.

There's a version of the NEXA story that Maruti Suzuki tells at every anniversary, and it's true. Launched on 23 July 2015 as a separate premium retail channel, NEXA went from 5% of Maruti's passenger vehicle sales in its first full year to a peak of 32% in FY2024 — 561,050 units in a single fiscal, up 52% year on year, more than most carmakers sell in India in total. Cumulative sales crossed 3.1 million vehicles within a decade. As a piece of retail strategy, it may be the most successful channel experiment by any volume carmaker anywhere.

Here's the version Maruti doesn't tell: NEXA was designed as a ladder, and it turned out to be a ceiling. The same architecture that let Maruti capture the ₹8–12 lakh buyer without diluting its value-brand equity now structurally prevents it from climbing above roughly ₹15 lakh. And — this is the part that matters for the next decade — the escape routes that were available in 2015 are no longer available in 2026. Maruti cannot push the Suzuki badge higher, cannot afford to launch a genuine premium brand, and cannot keep borrowing Toyota's engineering without commoditising itself. It is, in the strictest strategic sense, stuck.

The controlled experiment nobody designed on purpose

Strategy arguments about brand ceilings are usually unfalsifiable. You can always claim the product was wrong, the price was wrong, the timing was wrong. Which is why the Invicto is so useful: it is as close to a controlled experiment in badge economics as the Indian market has ever produced.

Hold the product constant. The Invicto is a Toyota Innova Hycross — same platform, same hybrid powertrain, same factory, same supplier set, same fundamental package. Hold the segment constant: three-row premium MPV, ₹24.97–28.61 lakh ex-showroom. Hold the network constant in the sense that matters: NEXA is Maruti's best retail experience, staffed and specified to compete above its station.

Change one variable. The badge.

Now put that against the denominator. FY2026 was Maruti's best year on record: 24.2 lakh units total, up 8.4%; net sales up 20% to ₹1.74 lakh crore; record profit around ₹14,445 crore; UV dispatches of 760,987, an all-time high. The company was not failing to sell cars. It was failing to sell this car, and only this car, and the thing that distinguishes this car from the one it is mechanically identical to is the three letters on the tailgate.

The Innova, meanwhile, continues to do what the Innova has always done. Same steel. Different badge. Different outcome.

That is the ceiling, measured.

What the ceiling actually is

It is tempting to read this as a product problem — the Invicto is a lightly re-nosed Hycross, the differentiation is thin, of course buyers went to the original. That reading is comfortable and it is wrong, or at least badly incomplete. It confuses the symptom for the mechanism.

The mechanism is that NEXA was built as a retail upgrade sitting on top of an unchanged brand. Everything NEXA changed was downstream of the purchase decision: the showroom, the lounge, the sales process, the delivery ceremony, the after-sales touchpoints. All of it good. None of it visible on the road.

A premium purchase above ₹20 lakh in India is not primarily a purchase of a better ownership experience. It is a purchase of a public statement, made once at the point of sale and then repeated every day for seven years in a parking lot. The showroom does not travel with the car. The badge does.

So NEXA solved the half of the problem that ends when the customer drives away, and left untouched the half that begins there. Below ₹15 lakh that trade works beautifully, because in that band the customer is buying a car and the retail experience is a genuine differentiator against rivals who offer none. Above ₹20 lakh the customer is buying an identity, and Maruti is offering them the identity of a company that also makes the Alto.

This is not snobbery, and it is not fixable with more marketing. It is the arithmetic of brand gradient. A badge's ceiling is set by the cheapest product it appears on, and Maruti's cheapest product is very cheap indeed. That is the source of its extraordinary strength across forty-plus years and the source of this precise weakness.

The three exits, and why each one has closed

Exit one: push the Suzuki badge higher. This was viable in 2015 and is not viable now, because the market moved and Maruti did not. In 2015 the ₹20 lakh-plus segment was small enough that missing it was a rounding error. In 2026 it is the profit pool. Almost all of the segment growth since 2020 has come from above the band Maruti dominates, and the brands that arrived there first — Hyundai, Kia, Mahindra, Tata, MG — have already set the reference points. Maruti is now trying to climb a ladder while three competitors are standing on it.

Exit two: launch a genuine premium brand. The Genesis manoeuvre: new name, new emblem, new network, new product, new engineering budget. Hyundai spent roughly a decade and several billion dollars on it, and Genesis is still not unambiguously profitable. Maruti's operating model makes this structurally harder than it was for Hyundai, not easier. Its margin comes from volume and parts commonality, and a premium brand is a deliberate attack on both. It also does not control its own product development — Suzuki does, from Hamamatsu, and Suzuki is a company whose entire global identity is built on small, affordable, brilliantly engineered cars. Asking Hamamatsu to fund a premium brand for one subsidiary is asking it to fund an argument against itself.

The window for this was 2015–2018, when the market was still forming and the capital cost was lower. It closed.

Exit three: borrow Toyota's premium. This is the exit Maruti actually took, and the Invicto is what it looks like. It is the cheapest of the three — no new brand, no new platform, no new plant — and its cost is deferred rather than avoided. Every Toyota-sourced product Maruti rebadges does two things at once: it fills a segment gap, and it teaches the market that a premium Maruti is a Toyota with the wrong badge. The first effect is worth a few thousand units. The second is worth considerably more than that, in the wrong direction.

Note the asymmetry, because it is the whole game. The Baleno-to-Glanza and Fronx-to-Taisor direction works: Toyota's badge lifts a Maruti product. The Hycross-to-Invicto direction does not: Maruti's badge lowers a Toyota product. The same alliance runs profitably in one direction and destructively in the other, and the difference is entirely brand gradient. Maruti is, in effect, running a business that proves its own ceiling twice a year in the sales data.

The one thing that changes the arithmetic

There is a version of the next decade where this resolves, and it runs through electrification — not for the usual environmental reasons, but for a structural one.

Premium in an ICE car is expensive to manufacture. It lives in displacement, cylinder count, NVH engineering, transmission refinement — hardware Maruti has no reason to own and Suzuki has no intention of developing. An EV moves the premium signal off the hardware Maruti lacks and onto hardware it can actually buy: interior materials, switchgear, seat construction, HMI, software, ADAS, acoustic packaging. Performance stops being a premium differentiator because it becomes free. The gap between a ₹12 lakh platform and a ₹25 lakh car narrows from an engineering problem to a specification problem.

Suzuki has the architecture — HEARTECT-e — and Maruti has now shipped its first product on it. Which means that for the first time since 2015, the hardware half of the premium problem is soluble on a Suzuki platform without Toyota's help.

That does not solve the badge half. Nothing in the parts bin solves the badge half. But it changes what a solution would need to cost, and it gives the argument a timestamp: the window is open for roughly one product cycle, while the Indian EV segment above ₹20 lakh is still being defined.

What to watch

Two tells, both observable from outside the company, both falsifiable within eighteen months.

The first is parts leakage. Watch whether e-Vitara interior components — the switchgear, the steering controls, the HMI stack — appear in the next generation of Arena products. If they do, Maruti has chosen commonality over separation, and NEXA remains a retail channel with a nicer lounge. If they are held back and treated as a NEXA-exclusive hardware set, Maruti has begun, quietly, to build a brand rather than a showroom. This is the clearest signal of intent available, and it will be visible in a press launch long before it appears in a strategy document.

The second is twin discipline. The Victoris and Grand Vitara now sit alongside each other the way the Baleno and Swift always have — related underneath, distinct in argument, aimed at different buyers. The XL6 and Ertiga do not; they are the same vehicle relocated to a different showroom. Which of those two patterns Maruti repeats on its next twin tells you whether NEXA is being treated as a brand with a reason to exist or as a distribution overflow valve.

NEXA is not a failure. It is a success that hardened into a constraint, which is a more interesting problem and a much harder one to admit inside a company celebrating its best-ever year. The ladder was real. The company climbed it. It is now standing on the top rung, holding a badge that cannot go higher, in the only part of the Indian market that is still growing.

Maruti Suzuki and NEXA

The Sub-Brand Gradient

Part 2 — Maruti's way out of the badge ceiling already exists. Its Chinese competitors built it, it costs a fraction of what a premium brand costs, and it can be reversed if it fails.

Part 1 argued that NEXA is a ceiling: a retail upgrade sitting on an unchanged badge, working beautifully below ₹15 lakh and structurally incapable of working above ₹20 lakh. The usual response to that diagnosis is the Genesis prescription — build a real premium brand, spend a decade and a few billion dollars, and wait.

That prescription is why nothing happens. It is too expensive to approve and too irreversible to pilot, so it dies in committee every year and the ceiling stays where it is.

The premise of this piece is that the choice between "do nothing" and "build Genesis" is a false one, and that the industry has spent the last six years demonstrating the third option at scale. Brand separation is not a threshold. It is a gradient, and it can be walked one rung at a time, with each rung priced separately and each rung reversible.

What the Chinese actually did

The standard Western read of Chinese brand proliferation is that it is chaotic — dozens of nameplates thrown at a wall. Look at the sequencing and it is close to the opposite: it is unusually disciplined, and the discipline is that separation is bought incrementally.

BYD did not begin with Denza and Yangwang. It began with Dynasty and Ocean — two series inside one brand, sharing the badge, distinguished by naming convention, design language, and showroom. No new emblem. No new legal entity. No new engineering budget. What it bought was the habit, in the customer's head and in the dealer network, that a BYD is not one undifferentiated thing. Only after that habit existed did Denza and Yangwang arrive as genuinely separate brands with their own emblems and their own price ceilings. The series split was the cheap rung. The brand split was the expensive rung. They were bought in order, years apart.

Geely ran the same sequence in the other direction. Zeekr was established first as the premium marque, and then Galaxy arrived — not as a brand but as a series-with-emblem, sitting below Zeekr and above the base Geely line. Galaxy gets its own badge treatment and its own retail identity while remaining, structurally, a series of a mass brand. It is precisely the intermediate rung that Western OEMs treat as not existing.

The mechanism in both cases is the same, and it is worth naming plainly: a sub-brand buys perceptual separation without buying structural separation. You get a distinct signal on the tailgate, in the showroom, and in the customer's mental model. You do not get a distinct platform, a distinct engineering organisation, a distinct dealer contract, or a distinct P&L. The cost falls by an order of magnitude. So does the risk, because a series can be folded back into the parent brand in a single model-year with no write-down.

The objection, and why it doesn't dispose of the argument

The obvious objection is that this is a Chinese phenomenon — an artefact of a market with unusual tolerance for new nameplates and a customer base with no inherited brand hierarchy. Import it into India and you get a rebadge with a different font.

Two counters.

First, the pattern is not exclusively Chinese; it is just currently most visible there. Honda has reserved its flattened, two-dimensional H for the 0 Series — a new emblem, derived from the old one, applied to one product family and withheld from everything else. That is exactly the manoeuvre: resemblance retained, difference communicated, no new brand created. Toyota has done the equivalent with the Crown, taking a nameplate with its own emblem and expanding it into a family that operates as a tier rather than a model. Neither company built a Genesis. Both bought separation on the cheap rung.

Second, and more important: India already has the customer behaviour. Indian buyers have proven for a decade that they will pay a substantial premium for badge signalling within a single manufacturer's range. The market does not need to be taught the concept. NEXA itself is the proof — it created a genuinely distinct retail identity that customers recognise and value. It simply stopped one step short of putting that identity on the car.

Three rungs, in order

The gradient for Maruti has three rungs, and they should be bought sequentially, each contingent on the last one working.

Rung one: a NEXA-exclusive touchpoint stack. Not a trim level. A hardware set — steering wheel switchgear, column stalks, HMI, door pulls, seat construction, the specific plastics and coatings — that appears on NEXA products and never appears on an Arena product. The e-Vitara parts bin is the obvious seed, because it exists, it was developed to a higher standard than the Arena set, and it has not yet been diffused across the range.

This is the cheapest rung in capital terms and the most expensive in political terms, for reasons I will come to.

Rung two: an exterior design language with a codified signature. Not a grille treatment, which is what most OEMs mean when they say design language and which is invisible from three of four angles. A lighting signature — a specific graphic, defined once, applied across the NEXA line, and legible at night from behind at a distance. This is the cheapest possible form of on-road brand signalling, because lighting hardware is already being redesigned on every model cycle anyway; the cost is the discipline of committing to a graphic and refusing to vary it.

Rung three: an evolved emblem, NEXA-only. Derived from the Suzuki S, not replacing it. The Honda 0 Series precedent is the template: the same letterform, flattened, simplified, reserved. A customer should be able to see the resemblance and simultaneously register that this is not the same badge as the one on a hatchback.

Rungs one and two are Maruti's decisions. Rung three requires Hamamatsu, and that is where this stops being a strategy question.

The real antagonist is internal

Every external competitor is a smaller obstacle to this plan than Maruti's own operating model.

Parts commonality is not a cost-reduction tactic at Maruti; it is the margin religion. It is the reason a company selling cars at Indian price points earns the returns it does. The entire supplier ecosystem, the entire service parts economics, the entire manufacturing footprint is optimised around the assumption that a switch is a switch across the range. Rung one asks the company to fork the parts bin deliberately — to hold back a better component from a product that could use it, and to accept a higher unit cost on both sides of the fork.

That is not a small ask. It is a request to spend margin on perception, which is the hardest kind of investment to defend internally because the return does not show up in the same line item as the cost.

Rung three is harder still. It asks Suzuki — a Japanese parent whose global brand identity is built on affordability, and whose emblem is one of the most recognisable letterforms in the industry — to permit an Indian subsidiary to redraw the corporate mark for its own use. There is precedent for regional emblem variation in the industry, but not much, and none of it involved a subsidiary this large asking a parent this protective.

This is the most interesting paragraph in the analysis and it deserves stating without hedging: the binding constraint on Maruti going upmarket is not the Indian consumer, not the competition, and not the product. It is the relationship between Gurgaon and Hamamatsu, and the fact that the strategy that is right for Maruti is orthogonal to the strategy that is right for Suzuki globally.

Why the window has a timestamp

The gradient argument would be true at any point in the last decade. What makes it urgent now is that the hardware half of the problem is temporarily soluble.

As Part 1 set out: in an ICE car, premium is expensive because it lives in displacement and mechanical refinement — things Suzuki does not build and will not build. In an EV, premium migrates to interior, software, ADAS, and acoustics, all of which are procurable. Performance ceases to be a differentiator because it becomes free. For a brief period — roughly one product cycle — a company with a good B-segment EV architecture and a willingness to specify generously can build a genuinely premium-feeling product without owning premium powertrain engineering.

Suzuki has HEARTECT-e. Maruti has shipped on it. The Indian EV market above ₹20 lakh is still being defined, which means the reference points are not yet set, which means a new badge entering that band is not fighting an established hierarchy.

Two years from now the hierarchy will exist and the argument gets much harder.

The tell

The falsifiable claim, and the reason this piece has a date on it:

Watch whether e-Vitara interior components leak into the next generation of Arena products. If the switchgear, the steering controls, and the HMI stack appear on a mass-market Maruti within the next two model cycles, the parts bin has not been forked and rung one has not been bought. Everything downstream of it is then decoration, and NEXA remains what it has been since 2015 — an excellent showroom attached to an unchanged badge.

If those components are held back, the gradient has started. It will not be announced. It will simply be visible in the spec sheet.

An Open Memo to the Board of Maruti Suzuki

Part 3 — four decisions, in the order they have to be taken.

To: The Board of Directors, Maruti Suzuki India Limited Re: The premium ceiling, and what it would cost to remove it Date: July 2026

You have just closed the best fiscal year in the company's history. Total sales of 24.2 lakh units, net sales up 20% to ₹1.74 lakh crore, record profit, record exports, record utility vehicle volume. On any conventional reading, nothing requires fixing.

This memo argues that the best year in company history is the correct moment to address the one thing the numbers do not show, which is that the company has no access to the only part of the Indian market that is still expanding in value terms. NEXA solved the retail problem and left the badge problem untouched. The Invicto — 3,247 units in FY2026, down 20%, last among nine utility vehicles, mechanically identical to a Toyota that sells perfectly well — is the measurement of that gap, and it is a measurement the market takes for you every month.

What follows is not a proposal to build a premium brand. That option is closed, for reasons of capital, timing, and parental strategy. It is a proposal to buy separation incrementally, on four decisions, in a specific order. The order matters more than any individual decision, because the first one is the only one that is expensive and the last one is the only one that is not yours to take.

Move One — Fork the parts bin

The decision: Establish the e-Vitara touchpoint stack as a NEXA-exclusive hardware set. Steering wheel switchgear, column stalks, HMI hardware, door pulls, seat construction, the specific plastics and coatings. It appears on NEXA products. It does not appear on Arena products, in this cycle or the next.

Why it goes first: Because it is the only move that costs real money, and because every subsequent move is worthless without it. Design language and emblems applied to a car that feels identical to a hatchback from the driver's seat are marketing, and the Indian customer above ₹20 lakh has demonstrated repeatedly that they are not persuaded by marketing.

What it costs: Higher unit cost on both sides of the fork, permanently. Loss of scale on two component families rather than one. An additional supplier qualification burden. A direct violation of the commonality doctrine that has produced this company's returns for four decades.

Why the board will resist it, stated plainly so it can be argued rather than avoided: This is a request to spend margin on perception. The cost appears immediately in a line item with a named owner. The return appears eventually in a segment the company does not currently participate in. There is no version of this proposal in which that asymmetry is comfortable.

The counter-argument is the only one that matters: commonality is what produces the margin and what produces the ceiling. They are the same mechanism observed from two sides. The company cannot keep the first and escape the second.

Move Two — A distinct NEXA exterior design language

The decision: Commit to a codified lighting signature — a specific graphic, defined once, applied without variation across the NEXA line, front and rear.

Why not a grille: A grille treatment is the industry's default answer and it is close to useless. It is visible from one of four angles, it is invisible at night, and it is the first thing a facelift changes. A lighting signature is visible from behind, at distance, in the dark, which is where the majority of a car's brand impressions are actually made in urban India.

Why it is cheap: Lighting hardware is redesigned on every model cycle regardless. The incremental cost of committing to a shared graphic is near zero. The expensive part is not money; it is discipline — refusing to let each model programme optimise its own signature, and holding that line across at least two cycles before the signature becomes recognisable.

The test: A NEXA product should be identifiable at night, from behind, at fifty metres, by someone who cannot read the badge. If it is not, the signature has not been codified; it has merely been styled.

Move Three — Evolve the emblem, for NEXA only

The decision: A NEXA-exclusive emblem, derived from the Suzuki S rather than replacing it. Same letterform, flattened and simplified, applied to NEXA products and withheld from everything else.

The precedent is not Chinese and should not be argued as Chinese. Honda has reserved a flattened two-dimensional H for the 0 Series — a derived mark, applied to one family, withheld from the rest. Toyota has done the equivalent in expanding the Crown into a tier with its own emblem. Both companies achieved on-road brand separation without creating a new brand, without a new dealer contract, and without a new engineering organisation.

Why this is the hardest of the four: It is not the board's decision alone. It requires Hamamatsu to permit an Indian subsidiary to redraw the corporate mark, and Suzuki's global identity is built on precisely the affordability positioning that this move is designed to escape locally. The company should expect the request to be understood, correctly, as an argument that the parent brand is a constraint in its largest market.

Which is why it goes third, not first. Ask for it after Moves One and Two have produced a product that visibly justifies it. An emblem request backed by a demonstrably differentiated vehicle is a product argument. The same request made in advance is a brand-management argument, and it will not survive the journey to Japan.

Move Four — Adopt a twin-model doctrine, and publish it internally

The decision: Codify which twin-model patterns are permitted and which are prohibited, and hold every future product programme to it.

The approved pattern — Victoris / Grand Vitara, Baleno / Swift. Shared underneath, genuinely distinct above: different exterior argument, different interior specification, different customer thesis. Each twin has a reason to exist that can be stated in one sentence without reference to the other.

The prohibited pattern — XL6 / Ertiga. The same vehicle relocated to a different showroom. This pattern does two kinds of damage simultaneously. It teaches the NEXA customer that NEXA is a distribution arrangement rather than a brand, and it teaches the Arena customer that the premium channel sells the same car for more money. It is the single most efficient way to spend the equity that Moves One through Three are attempting to build.

Why it belongs in a board memo rather than a product planning document: Because the prohibited pattern is always locally rational. Every individual instance of it fills a segment gap cheaply and adds volume in the quarter it launches. Only the cumulative effect is destructive, which means only the board can prohibit it. Product planning will re-derive the XL6 every eighteen months, correctly, on the numbers available to product planning.

What doing nothing costs

The company's current position is defensible for perhaps one more product cycle. Its volume base is secure, its cost position is unmatched, and its export business is now genuinely material.

What it forfeits is participation in the segment where the industry's profit is migrating, at the exact moment when electrification has temporarily made that segment cheaper to enter than it will ever be again. Premium in an ICE car is bought with displacement and mechanical refinement, which this company will never own. Premium in an EV is bought with interior, software, ADAS and acoustics, all of which are procurable, and Suzuki already has the architecture. That window is open for roughly one cycle, while the Indian EV market above ₹20 lakh is still forming and its hierarchy is not yet set.

The cost of doing nothing is therefore not measured in this year's market share. It is measured in the price of entry a decade from now, when the same decision must be taken against established competitors, with no structural discount available, and with ten additional years of Suzuki-badged hatchbacks setting the ceiling.

The company has spent eleven years proving that its retail experiment worked. The next decision is whether it was building a channel or a brand.

Chief financial officer Richard Molyneux's position on moving production there is nonetheless: "At our scale, it doesn't make sense for us to localize production of existing vehicles into North America."

So the 30,000 Defenders keep coming from Britain at 10%. What gets built in America is a set of new models, in segments JLR does not currently occupy, on somebody else's architecture.

That is not localisation. It is a product deal that happens to be assembled in America — and the existing volume, the volume the tariff is actually charged on, does not move.

The schedule that produces this

The two halves of the tariff regime were set independently and point in opposite directions.

What crosses the borderRateCharged on
Finished vehicle, Japan / Korea / EU15%vehicle value
Finished vehicle, United Kingdom10%vehicle value
Imported steel, aluminium and copper50%full customs value
Component wholly of steel or aluminium50%full customs value of the part
Derivative component, >15% metal content25%full customs value of the part
Non-USMCA components25%component value
Chinese components25%component value

Everything below the second row is charged only if you build in America. A manufacturer importing finished cars pays one duty, once. A manufacturer assembling in America pays on each imported input — and since April 2026 the metals duty applies to a product's entire customs value rather than to its metal content alone, so a bracket that is 20% steel by value is dutied at 25% of the whole bracket, not 25% of the steel in it.

The first charge is capped and predictable. The second compounds with the number of imported components in the bill of materials.

That is the inversion: the border charge on assembling a car in America can exceed the border charge on importing the finished car instead, and the more of the supply chain that sits outside the United States, the more reliably it does.

The offsets do not close it. A manufacturer assembling in the US could claim an import adjustment worth 3.75% of the aggregate MSRP of its US-assembled output from April 2025 to April 2026. From May 2026 that falls to 2.5%, and it is scheduled to keep falling.

The $7,000, and how much weight it carries

A major US automaker has put a number on the gap: $7,000 per car in favour of a Japanese or Korean import over a comparable vehicle built in the United States. It was reported by Autoline Daily on the arithmetic set out above — 50% on imported steel and aluminium, 25% on non-USMCA components, 25% on Chinese components, and further duties on top, against a flat 15% for the importer.

Three things about that figure need saying before it is used.

It is anonymous. The automaker declined attribution. There is no published method, no model, and no way to check which vehicle, which content mix or which duties are in the comparison.

It comes from an interested party. Detroit is lobbying the administration right now over the USMCA revision, arguing against a 50% US-content requirement its members estimate at $2bn a year each. A $7,000 competitive gap is precisely the number that argument needs. Ford's chief executive is separately calling for importers to be penalised. None of that makes the figure wrong; all of it means it was produced by someone who wanted a large answer.

And it is a different measure from the one usually quoted against it. Kelley Blue Book put 2025's tariff cost at $30bn industry-wide and the average MSRP rise at 10.4% — $5,000–$8,900 on imported vehicles against $1,600–$2,000 on domestic ones. That looks like a flat contradiction and is not. KBB is measuring realised price increases across the existing fleet, most of which is built by manufacturers whose US supply chains were established decades ago. The $7,000 is a marginal landed-cost comparison for a vehicle assembled from imported content today.

So the defensible claim is narrower than the headline and harder to dismiss: the schedule penalises manufacturers without an established American supply base far more than it rewards final assembly. It is generous to whoever is already here and punitive to anyone arriving — the reverse of the stated intent.

Which is why the JLR decision matters more than the $7,000 does. JLR has no stake in the USMCA fight, faces the lowest duty of any exporter, and reached the same conclusion with its own money.

Indicators

Whether the JLR–Stellantis MoU is signed before year-end, and whether the existing Defender is ever named in it. New models on Jeep architecture is a licensing deal. Moving the 30,000-unit Defender is localisation. Only the second would falsify the argument here.

Whether the derivative-parts rule is narrowed back to metal content. Charging 25–50% on a component's full customs value is the single provision doing most of the work; reverting it to metal content alone would remove most of the inversion.

Whether the US-assembly offset keeps declining past 2.5%. It is the only element of the schedule that pays for assembling in America, and it is shrinking on a published path.

Whether any manufacturer without existing US production commits to a new wholly-owned assembly plant. None has since the metals rule changed in April 2026. One would be a data point; two would be a trend against this reading.

The disconfirming signal. This piece argues the schedule discourages new entrants from building in America. If Audi restarts its plant, or JLR extends the Stellantis arrangement to existing high-volume models, or a Japanese or Korean manufacturer announces new US assembly capacity, then the tariffs are working as intended and the delay was negotiation rather than arithmetic.

Trade, tariffs and localisation

"You Can Never Localize Efficiently at 50,000 Units"

JLR's finance director has put the minimum efficient scale of car manufacturing on the record, as the reason his company will not build the Defender in America. That makes the Stellantis deal a scale story, not the tariff story it is being reported as. What follows from it is harder: JLR is not short of factories, the segment it would enter runs on an architecture the Defender succeeded by rejecting, and the last time JLR moved a rugged nameplate into adjacent space it took 86% of the Discovery with it.

On 13 August 2026, on Tata Motors Passenger Vehicles' first-quarter call, JLR chief financial officer Richard Molyneux was asked why the company would not simply build the Defender in the United States. He said:

"At our scale, it doesn't make sense for us to localize production of existing vehicles into North America. We sell circa 30,000 Defenders in the U.S. each year. We can never localize efficiently at 30,000 units, or even at 50,000 units."

JLR sells roughly 30,000 Defenders a year in America. So the answer is not that today's volume is too small. It is that two-thirds more volume would also be too small — and Molyneux said so rather than leaving it to be inferred.

Executives do not normally disclose their minimum efficient scale. It is the number that determines which markets you can enter, which products you can justify and how much of your business you must give to somebody else. Molyneux gave it away in a sentence, and most of the coverage led on a Jeep-based Defender instead.

It is not a tariff-localisation project

The tariff logic would run: JLR pays 10% on UK-built vehicles and 15% on EU-sourced ones, so it should move production to America and stop paying. If that were the plan, the vehicle to move is the one already selling 30,000 units a year at a duty JLR is visibly unhappy about — it has paid hundreds of millions of pounds in tariffs.

That vehicle is explicitly not moving.

What is happening instead is that JLR is adding volume it does not currently have, in segments it is not currently in, using an industrial base it does not own. The tariff is a reason to prefer American assembly for the new products. It is not the reason the products exist.

The reason the products exist is the 50,000-unit remark. JLR cannot reach efficient scale in America on Defender alone, so rather than build a plant it cannot fill, it is borrowing a plant that is already full and adding models to justify the borrowing. The deal solves a scale problem, and the tariff merely makes the solution more attractive than it would otherwise be.

If the problem is scale, why not use the plants JLR already has? It has spare capacity, and a great deal of it.

So the Stellantis deal is not about a shortage of factories. It is about where they are and what they are tooled for.

The spare capacity sits in Britain and Slovakia — on the wrong side of a 10% tariff, and on the wrong side of the dollar. And it is tooled for aluminium monocoques, which is the architecture the American rugged segment does not use. Filling Solihull or Nitra with a US-specific body-on-frame off-roader is not an option that exists, at any utilisation rate.

That is what makes Molyneux's remark coherent rather than contradictory. He is not saying JLR lacks plants. He is saying the marginal plant — a dedicated American line for one 30,000-unit nameplate, which is 9.7% of everything JLR sells — cannot be justified. Renting a corner of someone else's, in the right country and for the right architecture, can be.

While we are here: the 300,000 everyone quotes is that breakeven, not a target

The figure in the table above is worth stopping on, because it has escaped into commentary meaning something else entirely.

Since the Stellantis deal was announced, "300,000" has attached itself to JLR as an American sales ambition. JLR has never said it. The number is real and it is JLR's, and it describes the opposite kind of thing. Two statements, from the same June 2026 strategy release:

The 300,000"Enterprise missions to drive cost reductions of £1.7 billion to reduce breakeven towards 300,000 units over the next two years"
The US aspiration"Our aspiration, in the coming years, is to grow our US business to the size of the entire JLR business as it exists today" — P.B. Balaji, chief executive

The first is the global breakeven volume — the number of cars JLR must sell worldwide to cover its costs, currently 380,000 and heading down. It is a cost-structure target that gets better as it gets lower. The second is the American ambition, and it carries no number, no date and no unit figure.

The conflation is easy because the two land in almost the same place for unrelated reasons. Balaji's aspiration is for the US to reach "the size of the entire JLR business as it exists today" — somewhere between 308,000 and 324,000 depending on the measure. The breakeven target is 300,000. Depending on which you take, they sit between 3% and 8% apart.

The coincidence implies something uncomfortable. JLR is lowering the volume at which it breaks even to 300,000, and separately aspiring to sell about that much in America alone. The first is an admission that it cannot rely on selling much more than that globally. The second asks one country to become as large as the company that cannot. Both may be true; they are not obviously compatible.

Whether the American aspiration is reachable

Take Balaji at face value and give the aspiration a number — roughly 310,000 US sales, being the size of JLR today. Then model it generously: the existing US business growing 5% a year, plus a new Stellantis-built vehicle reaching 60,000 units at maturity.

And 5% is generous. US sales were around 126,000 in 2019, 99,000 in 2024 and 104,000 in 2025 — a cyclical business that has not beaten its 2019 peak in six years, not a compounding one.

So the aspiration is not reachable on market growth plus one vehicle. It needs a second and third Stellantis product, a Discovery recovery and the new Jaguar all working at once. That is possible. It is not a forecast, and JLR has not offered it as one — the company stated an aspiration and declined to attach a number to it; commentary supplied a number from the wrong line of the same page.

This distinction is not pedantry, given the history. From roughly 425,000 vehicles in 2013 JLR was widely projected to reach a million. It built the portfolio to match, peaked near 600,000, and never approached it — which is how a manufacturer ends up with a 380,000-unit breakeven and a £244m annual loss. The 2021 Reimagine strategy retired volume as the objective in favour of margin, and the evidence it meant it is in the mix: Range Rover, Range Rover Sport and Defender were 77.1% of wholesale in Q4 FY26 against 66.3% a year earlier, with guidance to an average selling price above £80,000.

A company that once destroyed value chasing a volume number is now being credited with a new volume number it did not set. If it is held to 300,000 American sales by people who have misread its breakeven target, the pressure runs in exactly the direction its own strategy spent five years escaping.

The reason nobody is reporting: the dollar

There is a second motive that has been almost entirely absent from the coverage, and it may be worth more than the tariff.

JLR sells heavily in America and manufactures in Britain and Slovakia. Revenue is in dollars; cost is in pounds and euros. That is an unhedged structural currency position, and it has to be managed with financial instruments that cost money and expire.

US production converts part of that into a natural hedge — dollar costs against dollar revenue, permanently, with no counterparty and no rollover. Molyneux's stated purpose was to get on the "right side" of the tariff barrier and to reduce dollar exposure. Both were given; only the first was widely reported.

For a company whose adjusted EBIT margin fell to 2.8% in the quarter to 30 June 2026, from 4.0% a year earlier, on revenue down 9.6% to £5.97bn, a permanent reduction in currency volatility is not a footnote. At those margins, exchange-rate movement is a material fraction of profit.

On body-on-frame versus monocoque, the honest answer is that nobody can check

The received view is that the full-size luxury buyer has moved to unibody, and that JLR — which builds the Range Rover on a monocoque and is good at it — should therefore expect a unibody architecture from Stellantis.

The public data does not support that, and more importantly it cannot settle it.

On the numbers above, body-on-frame outsells unibody in this segment by 92,605 to about 53,610, a ratio of roughly 1.7 to 1. The Escalade alone outsells the X7. Whatever is happening in the full-size luxury market, a flight from the ladder frame is not visible in it.

Every figure in that table verifies against manufacturer reporting except one: Mercedes publishes no model-level US sales, so the GLS is a third-party estimate that its own compiler notes is short a month. That understatement cuts against the argument here and is nowhere near large enough to change it — the GLS would have to be nearly double before body-on-frame stopped leading.

So: there is no public evidence that the full-size luxury segment is shifting away from body-on-frame, and the one figure that could overturn that is understated by a month, not by a factor.

Which leaves the architecture question genuinely open — STLA Frame, STLA Large, or something derived — and it should be reported as open rather than resolved by preference.

The current Defender achieved that by abandoning the thing the segment is otherwise built on. The L663 threw away the ladder frame, the utilitarian mission and the agricultural character of the original, and replaced them with an aluminium monocoque, road comfort and capability held as a credential rather than a daily purpose. It was a bet against the back-to-basics school — and the purest expression of that school, the Ineos Grenadier, exists precisely because its founder thought Land Rover had betrayed the Defender. It is a rounding error beside the thing it was created to rebuke.

So JLR is not short of evidence about which philosophy sells. It owns the evidence.

Which makes the question about the US product sharper than it looks. A body-on-frame Defender built on Stellantis hardware would be JLR re-entering, on someone else's platform, the one category it already leads — using the engineering approach it rejected in order to lead it.

There is a real case for doing it anyway. The G-Class demonstrates that a ladder frame carries pricing power a monocoque does not, and Defender has a stronger heritage claim to that ground than the G does. A US-only model in a segment the L663 does not occupy need not cannibalise anything. And at roughly 30,000 US units, the Defender holds about 5% of a category it dominates everywhere else — the American version of this segment is the one place its formula has not won.

The risk, then, is not the segment. It is the badge — which is the subject of the next two sections.

A large three-row model — which should be a Discovery

The full-size product is the one that keeps being described as a big Defender, and that is the version most likely to repeat 2020.

A large, three-row, family-carrying, genuinely capable Land Rover is not a Defender concept. It is the Discovery concept — precisely the vehicle the Discovery was when it sold 122,000 units, and precisely the space the Defender took from it.

Badging it Defender does three things, all bad. It puts the product directly on top of the Defender 130 and the Range Rover Sport. It wastes a dormant nameplate that already means exactly this, at the moment JLR is planning a sixth-generation Discovery anyway. And it stretches one name across a two-door rugged utility and a five-metre luxury three-row — which is precisely the manoeuvre the partner has just unwound. Jeep split one full-size vehicle across two tiers, Wagoneer below and Grand Wagoneer above; the family sells respectably at 45,040 units, but the two-tier structure was abandoned at the end of 2025 and everything folded under one name, four years after the split was launched.

Badging it Discovery does the opposite. It gives the revival a market — America, where three-row full-size is the default family vehicle rather than a niche — and it gives JLR a reason to build the Discovery somewhere its cost base works.

Cannibalisation risk: high if it is a Defender, moderate if it is a Discovery. The name is not decoration here; it determines which of JLR's own products the vehicle takes from.

What either one does for the brand

Two upsides, and they are different in kind.

The rugged model renews an authenticity JLR currently spends without replacing. The L663's entire proposition rests on a heritage of utilitarian capability that it does not itself embody — it is a luxury SUV trading on the memory of a work vehicle. That account is being drawn down. A genuinely hard-edged Defender, sold alongside the comfortable one, restores the thing the comfortable one is borrowing against. The G-Class works precisely this way: the credibility of the £250,000 version is manufactured by the fact that the vehicle is still, underneath, an army truck.

And American manufacturing changes what JLR is. It is currently a British exporter with a large dollar exposure and 28% of its revenue in one region. A US plant, a US-developed product and a US export programme would make it a manufacturer in its biggest market rather than a supplier to it — which is the difference between BMW's position in America and Audi's.

The risk sits in the same place as the opportunity. A Defender built in South Carolina on Jeep hardware is a Defender that a portion of the brand's customers will consider less authentic, not more. Land Rover has spent five years persuading people that a monocoque built in Slovakia is a real Defender. Whether that argument survives being made a second time, about a different architecture in a different country, is a brand question rather than an industrial one — and it is not obviously answerable in advance.

Company and industry studies

What a Missed Product Cycle Costs

Measured by volume-weighted portfolio age, BMW's American range averages 4.5 years and Stellantis's 7.7. The sharper number is the tail: **BMW sells nothing at all that is eight years old or more, and 43% of Stellantis's US volume is.** But this is not a German virtue. Audi's portfolio is older than Jeep's, and Audi fell 16% in a year — which is the cleanest evidence in the dataset that the clock is real.

A product cycle is a clock, and it is close to the only promise a carmaker makes to itself. Roughly every three years a model gets a refresh; roughly every six or seven it gets replaced. The clock is not a marketing convention. A showroom is a depreciating asset: a nameplate's share of its segment decays from launch, and the refresh resets the decay.

Miss the clock and the consequences arrive in order. Share erodes. Inventory builds, because the factory slows more slowly than demand. Discounts follow, because inventory has a carrying cost. Residuals fall, because the discounts are public. Then the brand is worth less, and the next product launches into a weaker franchise than the last one did.

Everyone in the industry says this. Almost nobody measures it. So here is a measurement.

For every nameplate selling more than 100 units in the United States in 2025, take the model year its current generation launched, subtract from 2026, and weight by volume. Volume weighting is the point: an unweighted average lets a 500-unit run-out count the same as a 200,000-unit core model, and Jeep, Audi and Dodge each have several of those.

What the correlation does and does not support

At brand level the relationship is strong and in the expected direction: r = −0.74 between weighted portfolio age and year-on-year change.

It is also six data points. The critical value for significance at n=6 is about 0.81. This correlation does not reach it. It is suggestive and it is consistent with the mechanism, and it is not proof, and anyone quoting it as proof is over-reading a sample of six.

Worse for the thesis: at model level the relationship disappears and slightly inverts. Across 35 nameplates, r = +0.13. Models four years old or newer averaged −9.0%; models eight years or older averaged +1.7%.

That result is real and it needs explaining rather than hiding, because two confounds are doing all the work.

The young cohort is disproportionately electric, and electric collapsed. Of the thirteen nameplates four years old or newer, four are battery-electric. They averaged −40.7%. The other nine averaged +5.1%. Being new was not the problem; being electric in the American market of 2025 was.

The old cohort is flattered by cannibalisation. The three old models that grew are all Stellantis, and all three grew by absorbing demand from dead stablemates. The Durango, on a generation dating to 2011, rose 36.7% to 81,168 units — while the Charger fell 94% and the Challenger 93%. It did not win new customers; it caught the ones falling out of the rest of Dodge. The Voyager rose 31% as the cheap version of the Pacifica. Neither helped the brand: Dodge still fell 28.1%.

So the model-level number measures powertrain and substitution, not age. Strip both out and the mechanism reappears.

Audi is the controlled test, and it breaks the German story

The premise that German premium makers are simply good at this does not survive the data. BMW is good at it. Audi is worse than Jeep.

Audi's weighted portfolio age is 6.5 years, identical to Jeep's, and 50.6% of its volume comes from product eight years old or more — a higher share than any Stellantis brand except Chrysler and Dodge. Its result was −16.1%, the worst of the six.

The Cherokee is the argument in one nameplate

Stellantis stopped building the Cherokee in 2023, left one of the largest segments in America empty for two years, and brought the name back in 2025 into a market that had learned to live without it. Result: 527 units.

Then the tell. The next-generation Cherokee is reported for 2029 — three years after the revival. A model normally gets six or seven years to earn back its development cost. This one is being replaced almost as soon as it arrives, which is what happens when a broken cadence and its recovery plan collide.

Share. Stellantis held 12.66% of the US market in 2019 and 8.80% by the end of 2023 — 3.86 points, or 30.5% of the position, in four years. US sales fell every year from 2018 through 2024, and 3.3% again in 2025 to 1,260,344, though the last two quarters grew.

The plan is also the admission

The plan is credible in shape. What it cannot do is act quickly, because cadence is the one thing in this industry money cannot compress. The penalty is incurred immediately and the remedy takes half a decade.

Why this matters beyond Stellantis

Jaguar Land Rover has just agreed to build its American future on this company's industrial base — new Defender-branded models, new segments, assembled at Stellantis plants, definitive agreement targeted around end-2026. That is a bet on Stellantis executing product on time, which is the specific capability whose absence produced a 527-unit Cherokee and a 124-day Chrysler inventory.

The counter is real: FastLane exists, the leadership that presided over the failure has gone, US sales grew in the last two quarters of 2025, and North America drove a 10% revenue increase in the second half. Stellantis may be a good partner precisely because it has just learned this at maximum cost.

But JLR is not buying the plan. It is buying the execution, on a schedule, in the market where the execution failed.

It holds the deepest balance sheet in the industry outside Toyota and has not bought a carmaker in twenty-five years. That looks like scar tissue from Rover. On the record of what acquisitions have actually achieved in this industry, it is the correct policy — and the current menu does not contain an exception.

BMW's automotive net financial assets stood at €45.5 billion in 2025 — more than Volkswagen's Automotive Division, more than Mercedes' industrial business, and more than Ford, General Motors and Stellantis hold between them.

Rolls-Royce was the better transaction and was barely a transaction. So the record is: one badge acquired almost free that became a permanent franchise, one brand inside a failed deal that became a growth engine, and one asset sold under duress. The thing that cost £1.79bn was buying the volume manufacturer. The things that worked cost almost nothing.

Toyota built — it renamed its largest Chinese R&D base, moved development authority to it, and folded in engineers from its three joint ventures. It did not acquire anything. Honda contracted — its Ye series uses Momenta for driver assistance and CATL for a cell-to-chassis battery platform. Those are supply agreements, not acquisitions.

Neither bought a company. Both got the capability. An acquisition would have added integration risk, political exposure and a balance-sheet commitment to precisely the fastest-moving part of the industry — the part where owning an asset is worth least.

The gap is real. Acquisition is not the instrument that closes it.

What BMW is actually doing, and why it is working

BMW registered 650,232 cars in the EU in 2025, up 4.1% against a market that grew 0.5%. It grew 9.8% in the E-segment and 10% below the D-segment — gaining share at both ends of its range.

It did that while retaining six-cylinder engines and rear-drive layouts, developing the Neue Klasse in Germany, and absorbing roughly 1.25 percentage points of tariff impact on automotive margin without cutting investment.

The €45.5 billion is not idle capital waiting for a deal. It is the reason the tariff hit was absorbable and the reason the Neue Klasse is funded internally rather than through a partner who would own part of the result.

And the market has been telling manufacturers something all year about how much technological leadership buyers are actually paying for. In the European F-segment, saloons fell 20.7% while SUVs fell 4.2% — and within saloons, electric flagships collapsed (Model S −70%, Model X −64%, e-tron GT −42%, EQS −36%) while combustion models in the same price band grew (Urus +239%, Continental GT +33%, Cullinan +23%, G-Class +16%, Panamera +5%).

Above a certain price, buyers chose the mechanical and the familiar over the technically advanced. A company whose answer to competitive pressure is "more of what we are known for, executed harder" is reading that correctly.

The verdict

BMW should not be preparing for a big acquisition, and the reason is not Rover.

The reason is that no acquisition in this industry has solved a product problem for the acquirer. NUMMI did not save GM. Rover–Honda did not save Rover. Daimler–Chrysler was unwound. Stellantis is the largest recent attempt at scale through merger and its industrial net position halved in 2025, to €6.7bn.

What worked for BMW was buying a name for almost nothing and building a business behind it, twice. What cost £1.79bn was buying a company.

Twenty-five years of restraint reads as trauma. Measured against the record, it is the policy the record supports — and the current menu contains no exception to it.

Indicators

Whether Stellantis sells Maserati and at what price. A distressed price for a badge is a different proposition from a company price, and the only version that resembles what has previously worked for BMW.

Whether BMW's E-segment share holds in 2026. The argument that organic execution is sufficient rests on it.

Whether any premium manufacturer buys a volume brand this decade. The rule holds until one does, and then it needs re-examining.

Whether net financial assets keep rising without deployment. A balance sheet growing indefinitely eventually becomes a governance question rather than a strategic one, and "we are being disciplined" has a shelf life with shareholders.

Company and industry studies

The Global Car Is Over. Development Is Going Local.

Toyota moved its Chinese development to China and put Chinese engineers in charge of it. Honda buys its Chinese autonomy stack and battery platform from Chinese suppliers. Stellantis gave up and licensed an Indian platform. The manufacturers that do not follow will not be global manufacturers — and the traffic is already running in both directions.

For thirty years the economics of the industry rewarded the opposite of this. A platform developed once and amortised across every market was the whole logic of scale, and the companies that did it best — Volkswagen with MQB, Toyota with TNGA — were rewarded for it.

That model is failing in the two largest growth markets simultaneously, and the responses to it are the clearest available signal of which manufacturers understand what has changed.

America proved the model first, and everyone has forgotten

This is not a new problem. It is a solved problem that has come back.

Japanese manufacturers established American design and engineering operations from the 1970s onward — Calty in California, Honda's American R&D in Ohio, Nissan Design in San Diego — for a reason that is now being rediscovered elsewhere: American vehicle preferences could not be served from Tokyo. Full-size pickups, three-row family SUVs and minivans were not scaled-up versions of Japanese products. They were different vehicles for a different use case, and they had to be designed by people who lived with it.

The result was a generation of vehicles that exist because local engineering existed — the Odyssey, the Ridgeline, the Tundra, the Sequoia. None of them would have emerged from a global programme run out of Japan, because none of them made sense as a variant of anything.

The lesson was learned, applied, and then treated as a solved American peculiarity rather than a general principle. It was the general principle.

China is forcing the same lesson, faster and at higher cost

The Chinese market now diverges from global norms on the three things that currently differentiate a car — powertrain, cockpit software, and driver assistance — and it moves faster than a global development cycle can track. Two Japanese manufacturers have responded, differently.

Toyota moved the authority. In August 2023 it renamed its largest Chinese R&D base Intelligent ElectroMobility R&D Center by Toyota — IEM by TOYOTA — and folded in engineers from the R&D operations of its three Chinese joint ventures: FAW Toyota, GAC Toyota and BYD Toyota. The remit is local development of all electrified powertrains, and local design of smart cockpits and driver assistance "that better fit the actual situation in China." Alongside it, an explicit cost programme built on developing a local supplier base and redesigning parts for it.

That is not a satellite office. It is the transfer of product decisions to the market they are for.

Honda went further and bought the stack. Its Ye series is a China-only EV line — six models planned by 2027, built by both its Chinese joint ventures, with the GAC Honda and Dongfeng Honda GT flagships shown at Auto Shanghai in 2025. The significant part is the sourcing. Honda is co-developing its driver assistance with Momenta, a Chinese AI firm, and its battery platform with CATL, including cell-to-chassis construction and LFP cells.

Honda did not localise its own technology for China. It adopted Chinese technology for China. Those are different decisions and the second is the more radical one — it concedes that on the differentiating systems, the local supplier is ahead.

India is the third instance, and Stellantis took the third option

The Indian version of the problem is cost rather than software. A vehicle engineered to European price points cannot be sold profitably at Indian ones, and localising a European architecture far enough to try has repeatedly failed the business case.

Stellantis confronted this directly for its next India-developed global Jeep and rejected both of its own platforms. STLA Medium was judged unviable to localise on cost. Smart Car — the platform Stellantis itself pioneered in India for global affordable cars — is front-wheel-drive only with dated electronics, and Jeep treats all-wheel drive as non-negotiable. A clean-sheet architecture was estimated at one to two billion dollars and five to seven years.

So Stellantis licensed Tata's ARGOS, the platform under the Sierra, with Tata also supplying the 1.5-litre turbo-petrol engine. The vehicle is targeted for 2028 and export to more than fifty markets. Tata licenses the architecture and supplies the engine; Jeep designs the body and interior; Tata has no co-development role and no stake in the product's commercial success.

That is the third response, and it is worth naming precisely because it is not the same as the other two. Toyota moved development to the market. Honda bought local technology. Stellantis bought a local platform outright and did no development at all.

What this means

The global platform is not dead as engineering. It is dead as strategy. Shared architecture will continue to underpin body-in-white, crash structure and manufacturing tooling, because the economics there are unchanged. What cannot be shared any longer is the part the customer experiences — powertrain calibration, cockpit software, driver assistance, and the cost structure underneath them. Those now diverge by market faster than a global programme can converge.

Local R&D has become a condition of market access rather than an optimisation. Not a legal condition — a competitive one. A manufacturer without local development in China is shipping a product a generation behind on the systems Chinese buyers evaluate first, and no amount of brand equity has proved able to compensate. Chevrolet's Chinese volume fell 92% between 2018 and 2024 while it competed with a substantially combustion line-up.

And the companies that get this right will look, for a while, like they are giving things away. Toyota handing product authority to Chinese engineers and Honda buying its autonomy stack from Momenta both read as retreats. They are the opposite. The retreat is the option that looks like prudence — keeping development at headquarters, shipping a localised variant, and discovering in two cycles that the local competitor now defines the segment.

Indicators

Whether any Western manufacturer establishes genuine Chinese development authority rather than a liaison office. Volkswagen's Chinese engineering arrangements are the test case with the most volume behind them.

Whether Honda's Momenta and CATL arrangements extend beyond China. If Chinese-developed autonomy or battery platforms appear in Honda products for other markets, the localisation has become a global sourcing decision — a much larger shift.

Whether Stellantis repeats the ARGOS structure elsewhere. One licensed platform is a market-specific solution. Two is an operating model.

Whether Chinese European R&D produces Europe-specific product rather than adaptation. Munich offices doing homologation and tuning are one thing. A model conceived in Munich for European buyers would be the same step Honda took in Ohio in the 1970s, and it would arrive with a cost base European manufacturers cannot match.

Company and industry studies

The Landlord OEM

There are now two stable positions in this industry and one unstable one. Most carmakers are standing in the unstable one.

The consolidation piece argued that the industry's restructuring is happening one layer beneath the firm — in platforms, powertrains, compute and plants rather than in corporate mergers. This piece is about what that leaves the firms themselves doing, because the answer is more binary than the sector's own language admits.

A carmaker owns two categories of thing. The first is industrial: plants, architectures, powertrains, E/E stacks, validation capability. The second is commercial: brand, product definition, customer relationship, distribution, service network. For a century these were bundled, because owning the first was the only way to obtain output and owning the second was the only way to sell it.

They have come unbundled. And once unbundled, there are exactly two positions that generate a return.

The landlord OEM owns the industrial layer and monetises it by supplying other people's brands. Its revenue is partly volume it does not badge. Its competitive advantage is capital efficiency: fixed assets that would otherwise sit at 40% utilisation are converted from a cost of competing into a source of income.

The tenant OEM owns the commercial layer and rents the industrial one. Its advantage is capital avoidance: it participates in segments it could never afford to engineer for, and its downside is capped at a purchase order rather than a plant.

The unstable position is the middle — a company that owns full industrial capability, uses all of it exclusively for its own brands, and cannot fill it. That describes a great many carmakers, and it is why the sector's utilisation numbers look the way they do.

Three cases, one structure

The framework is worth nothing if it only describes one deal. Here are three, in different regions, with different counterparties, arriving at the same shape.

Nissan India — the pure tenant

Nissan exited the RNAIPL manufacturing joint venture; Renault took the remaining 51% and became sole owner of the Chennai plant from August 2025 — a site with over 400,000 units of capacity that has built more than 2.8 million vehicles since 2010.

What Nissan kept: roughly 300 dealer outlets, forty years of Indian brand equity, an export programme, and a capacity reservation at a plant it does not own.

What Nissan gave up: any say in what gets built.

The evidence that this is the operating model rather than a transition is the product. Nissan's locally built Indian range is now the Gravite (a Renault Triber) and the Tekton (a Renault Duster on CMF-B). Every Nissan built in India is a Renault underneath. That is a complete tenancy, executed and on sale.

Renault — landlord and tenant simultaneously

Renault is the most instructive case precisely because it occupies both positions at once, which the framework predicts should be uncomfortable and which it visibly is.

As landlord: it owns Chennai outright and supplies Nissan from it. In Europe it supplies Nissan's entire light commercial range, builds the Micra EV at Douai on its own small-EV architecture, and is developing a Twingo derivative for Nissan through Ampere — designed by Nissan, engineered and built by Renault. Mitsubishi's European lineup is Renault architecture with Mitsubishi sheet metal.

As tenant: Geely holds 34% of Renault Korea in Busan, 26.4% of the Brazilian operation, and roughly 45% of Horse Powertrain — which is to say, Renault's entire extracted combustion and hybrid engineering organisation, a business valued at €7.4 billion with Aramco holding 10%. Renault Korea builds restyled Geely product.

Renault is renting out its European and Indian industrial base while renting in its powertrain capability and its Korean and Brazilian manufacturing economics. Both directions are individually rational. Together they describe a company that has decided the industrial layer is a market rather than an identity.

Stellantis India — the tenant that shouldn't be

The third case is the one that turns this from an observation into a framework, because the tenant here is not a subscale player. It is a company with fourteen brands and one of the industry's broadest platform portfolios.

At its 2026 Investor Day, Stellantis confirmed that a new Jeep SUV will be developed and manufactured in India on a Tata Motors platform, targeted for 2028 and exported to more than fifty markets across Asia Pacific, the Middle East, Africa and South America. Industry reporting identifies the architecture as ARGOS — Tata's modular platform, the one under the Sierra. Tata is also supplying the 1.5-litre turbo-petrol engine. It is a platform and powertrain supply deal.

The alternatives were evaluated and rejected, and the rejections are the interesting part:

  • STLA Medium — capable, multi-energy, global. Ruled out because localising a European-developed architecture for Indian price points was unviable.
  • A clean-sheet platform — ruled out at an estimated one to two billion dollars and five to seven years.
  • Smart Car / CMP — the PSA-lineage platform Stellantis itself pioneered in India for global affordable cars. Front-wheel-drive only, with dated electronics. ARGOS supports all-wheel drive, which is non-negotiable for the Jeep brand.

So a global OEM is renting an architecture from a direct competitor in that competitor's home market, and doing it through a fifty-fifty joint venture at Ranjangaon that has already built over 1.37 million vehicles.

The methodological point this case makes

There is a general lesson here that applies well beyond Stellantis, and it is the most useful thing in this framework.

A platform's ceiling is most credibly established by a competitor's platform choice, not by specification comparison.

You can argue about Smart Car's limits from a spec sheet indefinitely. Torsional rigidity, electrical architecture, packaging efficiency — all contestable, all subject to counter-argument about cost targets and market requirements. It is an unfalsifiable conversation.

Stellantis's own procurement decision ends it. The company that built the platform, that has every commercial and political incentive to use it, that would save enormously by using it, evaluated it for the exact market it was designed for and chose to pay a competitor instead. No spec comparison carries that weight, because no spec comparison is a decision with money behind it.

The corollary is worth internalising for any platform analysis: watch what OEMs buy, not what they claim. The buy is the assessment.

What makes tenancy survivable

The obvious objection is that the tenant OEM has hollowed itself out — that renting the industrial layer is the first step toward being a marketing department with a warranty obligation.

Sometimes it is. The distinction between a tenant that survives and one that is being dismantled comes down to a single question: is the rented layer the one the customer experiences?

Nissan India rents platforms and powertrains. Customers do not experience platforms. They experience the badge, the dealer, the ride tune, the interior, the service cost. Those remain Nissan's — thinly, but genuinely.

Stellantis rents an architecture and an engine for one product and retains Jeep's brand, its four-wheel-drive requirement, its design language, and its global distribution. Again: the rented layer is beneath the waterline.

The failure mode is renting the ADAS stack, the cockpit software, the HMI and the brand experience simultaneously — at which point the customer's daily relationship is with somebody else's product and the badge is decorative. That is the Wintel outcome, and it is a real risk, but it is a risk about which layer is rented, not about renting as such.

Badge engineering history is the most reliable predictor here. A company with a long record of selling other people's hardware under its own badge — Nissan with Renault in India, Stellantis supplying Toyota and Ram — has already established that its brand survives the arrangement. A company doing it for the first time has not.

Where this goes

Three predictions, each falsifiable.

Landlord revenue becomes a disclosed segment. At present, supplying other brands is buried inside automotive revenue. Once it exceeds roughly 10% of volume for any major OEM, investors will demand it be broken out — because it has a different margin structure, a different cyclicality, and a different multiple. Renault will likely be first.

Domestic platforms in emerging markets become export products. Tata has just demonstrated that an Indian architecture can be good enough for a global brand's fifty-market programme. That is a licensing business, and it will not stay exclusive to one customer.

The middle empties out. Companies that own full industrial capability, use it exclusively, and cannot fill it will resolve in one direction or the other within this decade — either by selling capacity to competitors or by giving up architectures and buying them in. There is no third outcome that earns the cost of capital.

The uncomfortable version of the conclusion: for most carmakers, the question is no longer whether to be a landlord or a tenant. It is whether they will choose, or have it chosen for them by whoever runs out of cash first.

Company and industry studies

One Layer Down

Marchionne diagnosed the disease and prescribed the wrong cure. Koji Sato is prescribing the right one — and it is not a merger.

I. The deck everyone quotes and nobody followed

On 29 April 2015, Sergio Marchionne put twenty-five slides in front of the analyst community and called the industry an addict. Confessions of a Capital Junkie argued that automotive had failed to earn its cost of capital across a full cycle, that capex and R&D were compounding faster than revenue, and that the reason was duplication: every OEM independently developing the same engines, the same transmissions, the same compliance hardware, the same crash structures, for products that competed on none of it. His own slides put up to €1bn a year of avoidable spend on duplicated engine and transmission development alone. He called the waste immoral.

The diagnosis was correct. It is still correct. The deck is quoted more today than it was in 2015 — Morgan Stanley has kept it in circulation for a decade, and every subscale OEM tie-up since has been narrated as a Marchionne vindication.

But the prescription failed, and it failed completely. Marchionne's cure was corporate consolidation: fewer OEMs, combined balance sheets, one R&D organisation where there had been two. Eleven years later there are more independent car companies selling in more markets than there were in 2015, not fewer. The mergers that were supposed to follow either did not happen (GM), happened and produced an integration gap rather than a merged entity (FCA–PSA), or collapsed in public (Honda–Nissan).

The mistake was not the diagnosis. It was the surgical plane. Marchionne assumed the firm was the unit of consolidation. It is not. The unit is the layer.

II. Toyota invented a job title for it

The clearest evidence that the industry has worked this out comes, characteristically, from Toyota — and it arrived as an org chart change rather than an announcement.

In April 2026, Kenta Kon became Toyota's chief executive. Koji Sato, who had held that role, moved up to Vice Chairman under a title the company created for the purpose: Chief Industry Officer. He had taken the chairmanship of JAMA, Japan's automakers' association, earlier in the year.

Read that structure carefully. Toyota separated the job of running Toyota from the job of restructuring the industry Toyota competes in, and assigned the second one to its more senior executive. No such title existed anywhere in the sector, because the activity it describes was not previously understood as a corporate function. It was understood as lobbying, or as antitrust exposure, or as somebody else's problem.

That is an institutional statement, not a rhetorical one. Companies do not invent C-suite roles for things they intend to talk about.

III. The 70,000 number is Sato's €1bn slide

Every consolidation argument needs one number that makes the waste concrete. Marchionne's was the €1bn of duplicated powertrain development. Sato's, presented in July 2026, is this: Japan's seven domestic automakers — Toyota, Nissan, Honda, Mazda, Subaru, Mitsubishi and Suzuki — collectively procure roughly 70,000 different wire harness specifications.

Seventy thousand. For a component that is flexible, awkward, resistant to automation, and therefore still largely assembled by hand. Sato's estimate is that standardising it, along with steel grades and resin specifications, could improve productivity by a factor of ten.

The number does its work the way the €1bn slide did: it converts an abstraction about industrial inefficiency into something a person can hold. And the mechanism behind it is the same one Marchionne identified. Seventy thousand specifications exist because seven companies each specified independently across decades of model cycles, each with a six-to-seven year product life and a ten-year service-parts obligation after production ends. Nobody chose this. It accreted.

But look at what Sato proposed to do about it, because this is the entire argument of this essay.

He explicitly did not propose mergers. He did not propose shared vehicle designs. He proposed standardising manufacturing standards and commodity components — harnesses, steels, resins — on the grounds that none of it is visible to the customer, and that Toyota and Honda can go on being ferocious showroom rivals while buying identical wiring. The stated purpose is to free engineers and capital for batteries and software. JAMA is running six further cooperation tracks alongside it: logistics, raw materials access, circular recycling, talent, autonomous-vehicle infrastructure, and the Japanese tax code.

Same diagnosis. Same waste. Different plane entirely. Marchionne wanted to merge the companies. Sato wants to merge the layer beneath the companies, and leave the companies alone.

IV. Why the front end resists consolidation

The obvious question is why Marchionne's version failed while this version is being institutionalised. Four structural reasons, none of which are about executive ego.

Brand equity is not additive. Merge two carmakers and you do not get the sum of their brand values; you get the larger one plus an integration problem. Every merger in the industry's history has destroyed nameplates. This is a real cost, it is not recoverable, and it does not appear in synergy models.

Distribution is legally sticky. Dealer networks are contractual relationships with statutory protection in most major markets. Two merged OEMs cannot rationalise their retail footprints at the speed the synergy case assumes, and often cannot rationalise them at all.

Regulatory approval is priced in political capital. Cross-border automotive mergers touch employment, national industrial policy and, increasingly, security review. FCA–PSA cleared. Honda–Nissan did not reach the point of trying.

The gains are back-loaded and the pain is front-loaded. Merger synergies arrive on the next platform cycle, four to seven years out. Integration costs arrive immediately. Any CEO with a five-year tenure is being asked to absorb the whole cost and hand the benefit to a successor.

The back end has none of these properties. A wire harness has no brand equity, no dealer contract, no national champion status, and no customer visibility. Standardise it and the gain arrives with the next model cycle for everyone simultaneously. This is why consolidation is happening there: it is the layer where the objections do not apply.

V. Minimum efficient scale moved

There is a deeper reason the plane shifted, and it is the most consequential paragraph in this piece.

For a century, minimum efficient scale in this industry was set by tooling. The press line, the paint shop, the transfer machine — capital equipment with a fixed cost and a volume threshold. That threshold is what made scale strategic, and it is what Marchionne was implicitly optimising when he argued for fewer, larger firms.

Minimum efficient scale is now set by compute and data. The expensive things in a modern vehicle programme are the E/E architecture, the software stack, the ADAS validation fleet, the battery chemistry programme and the OTA infrastructure. These have a radically different cost structure: enormous fixed development cost, near-zero marginal cost of replication, and — critically — no requirement that the user own the asset.

You cannot rent half a stamping plant. You can absolutely rent an autonomous driving stack, a battery platform, a cockpit domain controller, or a powertrain engineering organisation.

That single property change explains everything else. When scale lived in tooling, acquiring scale meant acquiring companies. When scale lives in compute, acquiring scale means acquiring a supply contract. Consolidation did not stop. It moved to where it can be bought by the unit.

VI. Three consolidations, three mechanisms

The layer is consolidating everywhere, but through three distinct mechanisms with three different sets of eventual owners. Conflating them is the most common analytical error in this area.

Japan: coordinated. Sato's programme, run through an industry association, with the largest player expected to make the first concessions. Elegant in theory. Its obstacle is not technical but distributional: standardising steel specifications means somebody discards existing blueprints, remakes moulds and reopens supplier contracts, and no participant wants to be first. The telling detail is that the first concrete standardisation move — Honda, Nissan and Mitsubishi coordinating on a common next-generation central computing unit, targeted for volume deployment around 2029 — does not include Toyota. The industry is standardising with or without the convenor.

Europe: distressed. No coordination, no convenor, just balance sheets failing in sequence. Bosch, ZF, Continental and Schaeffler announced more than 54,000 job cuts within a single year. Volkswagen has signalled lineup reductions of up to half. Consolidation here is being executed by turnaround funds and by Chinese strategics buying assets at distressed prices — which means the consolidated European supplier layer will, in significant part, not be European.

China: vertical plus merchant, simultaneously. BYD internalises — cells, semiconductors, and now shipping. Meanwhile CATL, Momenta, Huawei's HIMA arrangement and the domestic Tier 1s constitute a merchant layer selling complete subsystems to anyone. A Chinese OEM can choose vertical integration or full outsourcing, and both options are available domestically at scale. Nobody else has both.

Seen against that, Sato's proposal is not cost reduction. It is industrial policy — an attempt to build a Japanese merchant layer by agreement, because Japan has neither Europe's distress-driven consolidation nor China's state-adjacent capital formation. The context makes the urgency legible: Honda posted its first operating loss since listing, around ¥414bn; Nissan is in large-scale restructuring; and in May 2026, Chinese brands collectively outsold Japanese brands in Europe for the first time.

VII. The tenant OEM

Which produces the structure the next decade will be organised around, and which deserves a name.

A landlord OEM owns the layer: the plant, the platform, the powertrain, the architecture, and rents it out. A tenant OEM rents technical capability and retains what rental cannot supply — brand, product definition, customer relationship, distribution.

Neither is a failure state. They are two viable positions, and the dangerous place is between them.

The live proof case is in India, and it is more instructive than any European example because the tenant is a global OEM and the landlord is a domestic competitor.

Stellantis is developing its next Jeep for India on a Tata architecture — reported to be ARGOS, the platform under the Sierra, though Stellantis has confirmed only that the platform is Tata's. Not on Smart Car, the PSA-lineage platform that Stellantis itself pioneered in India for global affordable cars — that platform is front-wheel-drive only with dated electronics, and it cannot support what the segment now requires. Not on STLA Medium, which is capable but too expensive to localise at Indian price points. So a company that owns fourteen brands and a full platform portfolio is renting an architecture from Tata Motors, in a market where Tata is a direct competitor, because that is the cheapest route to a product that works.

That is the tenant model with the ambiguity removed. It also demonstrates something the platform-atlas view of the industry usually misses: a platform's ceiling is most credibly established not by specification comparison but by a competitor's platform choice. Stellantis's own procurement decision is stronger evidence of Smart Car's limits than any spec sheet.

Renault occupies both positions at once, which is the more interesting case. It is a landlord in India — full owner of the Chennai plant, supplying Nissan the vehicles it used to co-manufacture — and in Europe, supplying Nissan and Mitsubishi from its own architectures. It is simultaneously a tenant to Geely, which holds minority stakes in Renault's Korean and Brazilian operations and half of its extracted powertrain business.

VIII. The Wintel risk

The obvious objection to all of this is that the tenant OEM has outsourced the parts of the car that will eventually carry the margin, and that the merchant layer will do to automotive what Intel and Microsoft did to the PC industry: capture the value, commoditise the assembler, and leave the brand selling a box.

The objection is serious and should not be waved away. If the ADAS stack, the cockpit domain controller and the battery pack all come from merchant suppliers, and the customer's daily experience is increasingly mediated by software the OEM did not write, then the OEM's remaining contribution is styling, tuning, distribution and warranty — which is to say, a lot less than it used to be.

Two things make automotive a harder case than the PC, though neither is decisive.

The first is that the vehicle remains a physically integrated safety-critical system. Integration is genuinely difficult, genuinely differentiating, and cannot be fully bought — which is precisely why Sato's proposal is careful to standardise only what the customer cannot see.

The second is that no merchant supplier has yet achieved the position Intel held: a component that is simultaneously essential, undifferentiated in the customer's mind, and sourced from a monopoly. CATL is close on cells. Nvidia and Qualcomm are contesting compute. Nobody has arrived.

The window in which OEMs can decide what to keep is therefore open, and it is narrowing. The tenant OEMs that survive will be the ones that were deliberate about which layer they refused to rent. The ones that fail will be the ones that rented everything because each individual decision was cheaper than the alternative.

IX. Verdict

Marchionne was right that the industry was destroying capital through duplication, right that this was structural rather than cyclical, and right that it would have to stop. He was wrong about where the surgery happens.

Consolidation arrived. It came as merchant powertrain companies, standardised harnesses, shared compute platforms, rented plants and licensed architectures. It came without a single one of the mega-mergers he expected, and it is proceeding faster now than at any point since he made the argument.

That is the capital junkie's cure. It just didn't look like a merger.

Renault's Two Alliances

One is winding down. One is building up. They are running simultaneously, on the same balance sheet, and the crossover point is the story.

Renault is usually described as a company recovering from its Nissan dependency. That description is a decade out of date and it obscures the more interesting fact, which is that Renault has spent the last four years constructing a second alliance while the first one unwound — and that the second one is structured on entirely different principles from the first.

Alliance One is equity-based, governance-heavy, and shrinking. Alliance Two is asset-based, governance-light, and growing. Put them on the same board and the question stops being when does Renault escape Nissan and becomes when does Renault's Chinese-linked business become larger than its Japanese-linked business, and what that means for a company that is nominally European.

On the estimates set out below, the answer is approximately 2028–29.

Alliance One, in its terminal configuration

The equity unwind is largely complete and is worth stating precisely, because the headline numbers are routinely misquoted.

Renault held 43.4% of Nissan. Under the New Alliance Agreement effective November 2023, it transferred 28.4 percentage points into a French trust, holding 15% directly with capped voting rights, matching Nissan's 15% in Renault. The trust shares vote neutrally; Renault retains the economic rights until they are sold, and the sell-down has continued — the trust holding has since reduced. A March 2025 amendment lowered the required lock-up on both sides from 15% to 10%, which is not a small technical change: it is both partners buying the option to go lower.

The operational unwind has moved faster than the equity unwind, and in one direction that nobody predicted in 2019.

India is the clean case. In 2026 Renault agreed to acquire Nissan's 51% of Renault Nissan Automotive India Private Limited, taking full ownership of the Chennai plant. Nissan stays in India as a brand with a dealer network, buying vehicles — including the Magnite — from a plant it no longer owns. That is not a partnership dissolving. It is a partnership converting from joint ownership into a landlord-tenant relationship, with Renault holding the asset and Nissan holding the badge.

Europe runs the same structure in reverse gear. Renault, through Ampere, builds Nissan product on Renault architecture: the Micra EV at Douai on the small-EV platform shared with the R5, and a Twingo derivative for Nissan from 2026 — designed by Nissan, developed and produced by Ampere. Nissan's European light commercial range is Renault-derived across the board. Mitsubishi's European lineup is a Renault lineup with different sheet metal.

So the alliance is not ending. It is changing category. What used to be a mutual-development structure with shared governance has become a supply relationship in which Renault sells industrial capacity and platform access to two Japanese brands, and books the revenue.

The revision that changed the timeline

The first version of this model materially understated Alliance One revenue, and the correction is worth showing rather than burying, because it illustrates the failure mode of this entire category of estimate.

The initial figures were roughly €1.5bn (2025) and €2.5bn (2030). They were built from the visible, headline programmes and they omitted three asset classes:

  1. Nissan's European light commercial twins. Interstar, Primastar, Townstar — Renault-built, Nissan-badged, and individually unglamorous enough to be forgotten in a top-down estimate. Collectively they are substantial and they are stable, because LCV cycles are long.
  2. The Micra EV at Douai. A full passenger programme on Renault's small EV architecture, running through a French plant, that had been treated as a badging exercise rather than a revenue line.
  3. Mitsubishi's European platform twins. The Colt, the ASX, the Grandis — an entire brand's European presence supplied from Renault architecture, sitting outside the Nissan relationship and therefore outside the mental model.

Rebuilt from enumerated assets rather than from headline programmes, the estimates move to approximately €3bn (2025) and €5.5bn (2030).

Alliance Two, and why it is not a mirror image

The Geely relationship is not Nissan with different partners. It is a different instrument.

Alliance One was built on cross-shareholdings between the parent companies — an attempt to align two whole firms through equity, which produced two decades of governance friction and one criminal case. Alliance Two has no parent-level equity at all. Instead it is a set of stakes in assets:

  • Renault Korea (Busan) — Geely holds 34%. The plant now builds restyled Geely Group product for export.
  • Renault do Brasil (Paraná) — Geely holds 26.4%, its entry into South America.
  • Horse Powertrain — Renault and Geely at roughly 45% each, with Saudi Aramco taking 10%, the venture valued at €7.4bn. Renault's entire combustion and hybrid powertrain business, extracted from the parent and turned into a merchant supplier.

Nobody owns anybody. Each stake is separately negotiated, separately valued, and separately exitable. There is no board seat in Boulogne-Billancourt and no French trust in Hangzhou.

This is the same structural shift visible across the industry: from consolidation at the level of the firm to consolidation at the level of the asset. It is cheaper, faster, and considerably more reversible — and it does not require either party to pretend they have a shared destiny.

What Renault actually becomes

Read the two alliances together and the strategic identity is clearer than Renault's own communications make it.

Renault is becoming an industrial and architectural landlord with a retail brand attached. It owns plants (Chennai, Douai, Busan in part, Paraná in part), it owns architectures (the small EV platform, CMF-B, the Horse powertrain portfolio), and it increasingly monetises them by supplying other people's brands. Nissan, Mitsubishi, and Geely are all, in different configurations, tenants.

Two consequences follow, and they point in opposite directions.

The favourable one: landlord revenue is structurally less cyclical and less capital-hungry at the margin than brand revenue. Renault is converting fixed assets from a cost of competing into a source of income, which is exactly the right move for a mid-scale OEM that cannot win a global volume war.

The unfavourable one: landlords do not control the product roadmap, and a landlord that also competes with its tenants is in an unstable position. Renault sells Nissan the architecture for the Micra and competes with the Micra. It builds Geely product in Busan and competes with Geely in Europe. That is manageable while the tenants are subscale in the relevant markets. It becomes considerably less manageable around the point where the Geely-linked stream crosses the alliance stream — which is to say, around 2028–29.

The geography

The atlas is the argument, so it is worth reading as geography rather than as a list.

France — Douai, Maubeuge, Sandouville. Alliance One production, increasingly Nissan-badged output from French plants. Spain — Palencia, Valladolid. Core Renault volume and the European small-car base. Morocco — Tangier, Casablanca. The cost anchor for the entire European range. Turkey — Bursa. Long-cycle volume, largely outside both alliances. Brazil — Curitiba. Now partially Geely-owned; the first site where Alliance Two took physical form in the Americas. India — Chennai. Formerly joint, now wholly Renault, supplying a brand that used to be a co-owner. Korea — Busan. Partially Geely-owned, building Geely-derived product for export.

Plot the ownership changes chronologically and the pattern is unmistakable: every site that changed hands since 2023 moved away from joint Japanese ownership and toward either full Renault control or partial Chinese ownership. There is no counterexample.

What to watch

The 10% floor. Both sides now have the option to reduce cross-shareholdings from 15% to 10%. Whoever exercises it first, and whoever buys the shares, tells you more about the alliance's real trajectory than any joint statement.

Whether Busan or Paraná gets a second Geely product. One restyled model is a capacity deal. Two is a platform commitment, and it pulls the crossover forward.

Whether Nissan sources anything from Renault outside Europe and India. If the supply relationship stays confined to those two regions, it is a legacy wind-down being monetised. If it extends — to ASEAN, to South America — then Renault has successfully converted the alliance into a merchant business, and the wind-down framing was wrong all along.

The Tenant Empire

Geely stopped buying carmakers six years ago. What it does now is rent them — and the arrangement is better than ownership in almost every respect that matters.

On 23 July 2026, at a plant in Almussafes, outside Valencia, Ford and Geely announced a joint venture. Ford takes 66%, Geely 34%. Geely pays around €221 million, which values the whole factory at roughly €650 million. Operations begin in the first half of 2027 and vehicle production in 2028, at which point the site will build five models: the existing Kuga, a new compact Bronco, a crossover jointly developed by the two companies, and two Geely vehicles. The Spanish workforce transfers to the new entity. Geely will operate out of Body 3, the plant's newest and least-used building.

It is Geely's first manufacturing base in Europe. It arrived without a single brand changing hands.

That last sentence is the whole story, and it is the reason this deal is more instructive than any of Geely's famous acquisitions. Sixteen years ago Geely bought Volvo Cars from Ford for $1.8 billion. This week it rented a third of Ford's Spanish factory for €221 million. Same counterparty, one-eighth the price, and — depending on how you count — a comparable amount of European industrial access.

Act One: the acquisition era, 2006–2019

The first Geely was a buyer, and the purchases were genuinely bold rather than opportunistic.

Volvo Cars, 2010, $1.8bn from Ford. The transaction that made Geely's name, and still the best-executed acquisition of a Western carmaker by a Chinese company. Geely bought a distressed premium brand, left it substantially alone, funded the CMA architecture that Volvo then developed, and took the technology back. Whether you read this as Geely buying a brand or Geely buying an engineering organisation determines everything you conclude about what came next.

The Daimler stake, 2018. Roughly 9.7%, acquired through derivatives, announced as a fait accompli. Not control — access. A seat at the table of the company that defines the premium segment globally.

Proton and Lotus, 2017. A volume brand with Southeast Asian manufacturing and market access, and a British sports car marque with disproportionate engineering credibility relative to its revenue.

smart, 2019. Structured as a 50:50 joint venture with Daimler rather than a purchase — which, in retrospect, is the transitional deal. Geely got a European premium small-car brand without buying it, with the seller retaining half the exposure. Everything after 2019 is an elaboration of that structure.

Read as a portfolio, Act One bought three things: engineering capability, brand credibility, and market access. Read as a balance sheet, it also bought pension liabilities, legacy plants, union agreements, dealer contracts, and the obligation to keep marginal businesses alive.

Geely appears to have concluded that the first list was worth having and the second was not.

Act Two: the tenancy doctrine, 2022–present

Since 2022 the pattern has been consistent enough to be called a doctrine. Geely takes a minority stake in a specific asset, brings product to fill that asset's unused capacity, and leaves ownership, liabilities, and the balance-sheet burden with the incumbent.

The Horse Powertrain case is the purest illustration: Renault and Geely at roughly 45% each, Saudi Aramco at 10%, the venture valued at €7.4 billion. Geely did not buy Renault's engine business. It co-created a merchant supplier out of it, giving Geely access to combustion and hybrid engineering it can sell to third parties, while Renault removed a declining capital burden from its own accounts. Both parties describe this as a strategic partnership. Both parties are correct, and both are describing a landlord-tenant arrangement in which the tenant improved the property.

Why renting beats buying

The tenancy doctrine is not a compromise forced by capital constraints or political resistance. On the specific things Geely wants, it is straightforwardly the better instrument.

Tariffs and origin. The binding constraint on Chinese OEMs in Europe is not demand. It is the tariff regime and the political tolerance for imported Chinese vehicles. A car built at Almussafes by a Ford-controlled joint venture with a Spanish workforce is a European car. Buying a European brand does not achieve this. Renting European capacity does, and it does so faster than a greenfield plant, without the announcement risk of a Chinese-owned factory in the EU.

Capacity is the cheapest asset in the industry right now. Almussafes has roughly half a million units of annual capacity and has been running at something like 30%, with output below 100,000 vehicles by 2025 against a historical peak above 400,000. Ford's European share fell to 2.2% in June from 2.9% a year earlier. In that condition, unused capacity is not an asset the owner can monetise — it is a fixed cost they cannot escape. Geely is buying it at the price of a distressed liability, and the seller is grateful.

Liabilities stay behind. This is the load-bearing point. When Geely bought Volvo, it bought Volvo's obligations. When Geely takes 34% of a joint venture operating an existing plant, the pension exposure, the redundancy obligations, the environmental liabilities and the legacy dealer contracts stay predominantly with the majority holder. Geely acquires the product plan and the industrial access. It does not acquire the past.

Reversibility. A minority stake in a single asset can be exited. A brand cannot. Geely has retained the option value that Act One spent.

The trade-off is real and should be stated: a tenant does not control the roadmap, cannot unilaterally reallocate capacity, and shares the upside. Geely appears to have decided that for manufacturing footprint — as opposed to engineering capability, which it still buys outright — control is an expensive luxury.

The contrast that proves it is a choice

BYD is the counterexample, and it is a genuine one rather than a rhetorical convenience.

BYD builds. Its Brazilian operation at Camaçari sits on a site Ford abandoned, but BYD took the asset outright rather than entering a joint venture with the departing owner. Its European strategy is greenfield and wholly owned. Where Geely buys a third of somebody else's plant, BYD buys the whole plant, or builds a new one.

Two Chinese OEMs, comparable ambition, opposite instruments. BYD is accumulating owned capacity and accepting the fixed-cost exposure that comes with it, betting on volume growth to fill it. Geely is accumulating access and leaving the exposure with someone else, betting that access is sufficient.

Both are defensible. They are not the same strategy, and the industry's habit of treating "Chinese expansion into Europe" as a single phenomenon obscures the most interesting structural divergence in the sector.

Ford, twice

The symmetry deserves its own section because it is not a coincidence and it is not merely satisfying.

In 2010, Ford was a company in retreat from premium Europe, selling assets to fund a turnaround. It sold Volvo Cars to Geely for $1.8 billion, and the transaction was read at the time as a distressed European brand passing to an unproven Chinese buyer.

In 2026, Ford is a company in retreat from volume Europe, with a plant it cannot fill and a market share that has fallen to 2.2%. It has sold Geely 34% of that plant for €221 million and will co-develop a crossover with it.

Sixteen years apart, the same seller, the same buyer, and a complete inversion of what was on the table. In 2010 Geely needed a brand and Ford had one to spare. In 2026 Geely needs European ground and Ford has half a factory going unused. Neither company has changed its fundamental position — Ford is still selling what it can no longer afford to keep, and Geely is still buying what it cannot build fast enough. What changed is the definition of the valuable asset. In 2010 it was a brand. In 2026 it is a building with the right postcode.

What to watch

Whether the two Geely models at Valencia are Geely-badged. If they are, this is a manufacturing base and the tenancy is straightforward. If they arrive under a European brand, the doctrine has advanced a stage further: Geely will be renting the badge as well as the building.

Whether a fifth deal follows the same template. Four is a pattern. Five, with a new counterparty in a new region, makes it the standard instrument, and every underutilised Western plant becomes a negotiation waiting to happen.

Whether any host tries to reverse the flow. Every deal so far has moved capacity access toward Geely and liability retention toward the incumbent. The first Western OEM to take a minority stake in a Chinese asset on comparable terms would tell us the arrangement is genuinely mutual. Until then, the direction is one-way, and the word for that is not partnership.

Nissan India Is Already a Tenant. The Only Question Left Is Who the Next Landlord Is.

Every Nissan built in India is now a Renault underneath. That is not a transitional state — it is the operating model, and it has an obvious next move.

Most analysis of Nissan India is still written as though the company is deciding what to do. It isn't. It decided, the decision is executed, and the results are on sale.

Nissan exited the Chennai manufacturing joint venture. Renault acquired the remaining 51% of RNAIPL and became sole owner from August 2025, consolidating a plant that has produced over 2.8 million vehicles and 4.6 million engines and gearboxes since 2010, with capacity above 400,000 units. Nissan retains a dealer network, a brand, an export programme, and a production capacity reservation at a factory it no longer owns.

Then look at what Nissan has actually launched in India since. The Gravite — a sister car to the Renault Triber. The Tekton — Nissan's version of the Duster, on CMF-B. That is the entire locally built Nissan portfolio, and every unit of it is a Renault product with a different badge.

This is not a company weighing whether to become a tenant. It is a tenant, its lease is signed, and its landlord is a company it used to co-own the building with.

What the tenancy is actually worth

Read plainly, the structure is better for Nissan than the headlines suggested at the time.

Nissan produced roughly 99,000 units at Chennai in FY24 — around 28,000 for the domestic market and 71,000 for export — and has targeted roughly 100,000 of each. It had committed €700 million to new models and network expansion, the large majority of it already deployed. The manufacturing exit did not cost it access; it converted a capital obligation into a purchase order.

What Nissan gave up is the ability to decide what the plant builds. What it kept is a national dealer network of roughly 300 outlets, a brand with forty years of Indian equity, and an export licence to markets where Nissan's badge outperforms Renault's.

That combination — distribution and brand without manufacturing or architecture — is a coherent position. It is only a weak one if the tenant has a single landlord.

The constraint, stated precisely

Nissan's Chief Performance Officer, Guillaume Cartier, confirmed within the last month that a Nissan-platform vehicle could be built at RNAIPL, and that Nissan holds a capacity reservation there. Separately, Nissan management has said it is open to contract manufacturing or collaboration with other automakers in India, with no active negotiations yet and no restrictions on who it would evaluate.

Both statements are usually reported as reassurance. Read together they are something more interesting: Nissan is telling the market that its Renault arrangement is non-exclusive, and that it intends to keep it that way.

The reason is structural. A tenant with one landlord is not a tenant — it is a subsidiary with extra steps. Its product cadence, its segment coverage and its cost position are all set by a company that competes with it in the same showrooms. Nissan's Indian range is currently defined entirely by which Renault products exist. If Renault does not build a midsize SUV, Nissan does not sell one.

The only way out is a second supplier. Which makes "who else could build cars for Nissan in India" not a speculative question but the central operating question of the next three years.

Ranking the candidates, on structural fit rather than plausibility

1. Stellantis — the strongest fit by a distance.

The logic is capacity against volume. Stellantis operates three Indian manufacturing positions — Thiruvallur (the CK Birla joint venture), Hosur (powertrain, positioned as a global ICE export hub), and Ranjangaon (the 50:50 Tata JV, FIAPL, which has built over 1.37 million vehicles and currently assembles four Jeep and three Tata models). Citroën's Indian retail volumes are a small fraction of what that footprint can absorb.

That is the same asymmetry Renault had, in reverse: Stellantis has plants and no volume; Nissan has volume potential and no plants.

The precedent exists on both sides, which is what makes this structurally defensible rather than speculative. Nissan has a long badge-engineering history with Renault product in India specifically — Terrano from Duster, and now Tekton and Gravite. Stellantis has supplied badge-engineered vehicles to non-Stellantis brands globally for years: the Toyota ProAce, the Ram ProMaster City. Neither party would be doing anything it has not done before.

The product fit is also better than it first appears. Citroën's Indian C-Cubed products — C3, Basalt, C3 Aircross — sit on the Smart Car platform in a cost-down configuration built specifically for Indian price points. That is precisely the segment where Nissan's Indian range is thinnest and where Renault has nothing to lend it.

2. Volkswagen Group India.

Chronic underutilisation at Chakan and Pune, a well-established India 2.0 architecture in MQB-A0-IN, and a demonstrated willingness to look for a partner — the Mahindra discussions went a long way before failing. The platform is genuinely competitive.

The obstacle is that VW's Indian cost position has never been strong enough to make it a natural contract manufacturer, and the group's decision-making on India has been slow in a way that a tenant relationship cannot tolerate.

3. Force Motors.

The cleanest operational fit and the wrong scale. Force has decades of proven contract assembly for global OEMs, no competing passenger-car brand ambition, and no channel conflict whatsoever. It simply cannot deliver the volumes Nissan needs to justify a national dealer network.

Viable for a niche or a single body style. Not viable as a second pillar.

4. Tata Motors.

The capacity exists, the architecture is now demonstrably good enough for a global OEM — Stellantis is putting a Jeep on ARGOS — and the cost position is the best in the market.

The problem is that Tata competes with Nissan directly in every segment Nissan would want to enter, and unlike the Stellantis–Tata JV, there is no existing structure to contain that conflict. Tata is a landlord to Stellantis because Stellantis brings a premium brand into segments Tata does not contest. Nissan brings a mainstream brand into segments Tata dominates. The structural logic that makes Tata–Stellantis work is the same logic that makes Tata–Nissan awkward.

5. Chinese-linked entrants.

Technically the most capable option and politically the least available. Any arrangement involving Chinese-owned capacity in India runs into an approval regime that has not meaningfully loosened. Worth monitoring, not worth planning around.

Why Stellantis and Nissan is the deal that should happen

Set the two companies' Indian positions side by side and the complementarity is close to exact.

Each company's weakness is the other's surplus. Nissan needs a second platform source and cheap capacity. Stellantis needs volume through its Indian plants and a retail network that Citroën has never built.

And critically, neither would be conceding much. Stellantis is already comfortable supplying vehicles to brands it does not own. Nissan is already comfortable selling vehicles it did not engineer. The cultural obstacle that kills most such arrangements — the reluctance to admit dependence — has already been overcome by both parties, separately, in public.

The falsifiable version

Predictions in this area are usually unfalsifiable, so here is the specific form the thesis takes.

If it happens, it will start with one body style, not a range. A single Citroën-derived product, Nissan-badged, aimed at a segment where Nissan currently has nothing and Renault cannot supply. A sub-4-metre SUV or a compact crossover is the obvious candidate.

It will be announced as a supply agreement, not a partnership. The tenancy doctrine — visible in Renault–Geely, Ford–Geely, and Stellantis–Tata — is that these deals are now structured as asset-level or product-level contracts rather than alliances. Nobody is going to take equity in anybody.

The tell will be a capacity announcement at Thiruvallur or Hosur before any product announcement. Utilisation moves first; badges follow.

And the disconfirming signal is equally clear: if Nissan's next Indian launch is a third Renault derivative, the second-landlord thesis is wrong for this cycle, and Nissan has accepted single-supplier dependence as the price of capital discipline.

Infiniti Cannot Fill Its Showrooms. Alpine Does Not Have Any.

Infiniti has lost 95,345 annual US sales between 2017 and 2024 and is folding dealers into Nissan stores. Alpine plans a 2027 US launch on 8,428 European registrations. The obvious arrangement between them is reportedly not the one being made.

Two members of the same alliance have complementary problems in the United States, and the way they are choosing not to solve them together is the more informative part.

Infiniti has retail capacity it cannot fill. Nissan's premium brand delivered 153,415 vehicles in the US in 2017. By 2024 that had fallen to 58,070 — a decline of 62% — and 2025 has been pacing closer to 50,000, with second-quarter volume down 12.7% year on year. The brand has discontinued the QX50 and QX55 without direct replacements, and has begun closing standalone showrooms and co-locating dealers inside Nissan stores.

Alpine has a brand with no retail. Renault's performance marque registered 8,428 cars in the European Union in 2025, against 3,759 in 2024. It has confirmed a US entry for 2027 and a seven-model line-up.

The scale gap is the first thing to state plainly. Infiniti's decline between 2017 and 2024 — 95,345 annual units — is 11.3 times the size of Alpine's entire European business. Measured against 2025's pace it is closer to 103,000.

Infiniti's US volume fell from 153,415 to approximately 50,000 between 2017 and 2025, so throughput per outlet is down about 67% whatever the denominator, because the denominator has not tripled. On the mid-range count of 211 dealers, a store selling around 727 cars in 2017 is selling around 237 today.

That is the sense in which the capacity is available: not that the buildings are empty, but that each one is doing roughly a third of the business it was scaled for.

The arrangement that is not happening

The structurally obvious move is for Alpine to enter through the Nissan and Infiniti network. The two companies are alliance partners. Alpine has no US retail at all and no realistic means of building it for a two-thousand-unit brand.

Reporting indicates Alpine considered exactly this and has moved away from it. The brand is now described as in talks with AutoNation to establish an independent US retail presence rather than using alliance channels.

That decision is worth more attention than the launch date. Renault appears to prefer renting retail from an unaffiliated US dealer group over renting it from the partner it has held cross-shareholdings with for twenty-six years.

Several readings are available and the data does not separate them:

  • Brand contamination. Infiniti is a declining marque consolidating into mainstream showrooms. An Alpine counter inside a Nissan store that also houses Infiniti is three brands deep and premium in none of them.
  • Alliance drift. The Renault–Nissan cross-shareholding has been reduced to 15% each with the required lock-up lowered to 10%, and operational cooperation has been converting into arm's-length supply agreements. A joint retail venture is a deeper commitment than either party is currently making elsewhere.
  • Control. AutoNation offers negotiated terms with a counterparty that has no competing premium brand. The Nissan network does.

If the third reading is correct, the interesting conclusion is not about Alpine at all. It is that alliance membership no longer confers a retail advantage over what a French company can buy on the open market in America.

The product has already moved once

Alpine's US plan was originally mid-size and large electric crossovers. Reporting now indicates the brand is weighing entry with the next-generation A110 instead, and that the car — previously electric-only — has been adapted to offer a combustion option.

The stated reasons are US tariff exposure and the reduction of American EV incentives. A brand with 2,236 units of relevant annual volume is not positioned to absorb either, so the pivot is rational. It also means the US programme has changed both its product and its powertrain since being announced, while the 2027 date, first mentioned in 2023, has held.

Indicators

Whether the AutoNation arrangement is confirmed or lapses. It is reported rather than announced. A signed retail agreement would settle how Alpine intends to reach American buyers; a return to Nissan channels would indicate the alliance retains more operational value than the current direction suggests.

Whether the five-model Infiniti programme is delivered. Nissan has committed to five new models over five years — the QX65 in 2026, then a midsize hybrid SUV, a performance V6 sedan and two large hybrid SUVs. That is a network being refilled, not abandoned, and it substantially weakens the premise of this piece. Note also the powertrain shift: earlier reporting described the renewal as two electric models.

Whether the 2027 date holds. It has been in place since 2023 through at least one product and powertrain change.

Whether the A110 successor reaches the US with a combustion engine. That is the clearest signal of how Alpine reads American demand, and it would put a French performance brand into the US on the powertrain it had publicly abandoned.

The German groups have carried nine to eighteen large nameplates each on the strength of Chinese joint ventures, North American plants and Gulf and Korean export channels; Stellantis currently carries three.

Capital exposure. Stellantis funds 13% of the programme, with Dongfeng carrying €870 million. The arrangement resembles a supply agreement more than a joint development: the industrial layer is rented, the badge retained, and the downside limited to a purchase order rather than a plant.

Distribution. Peugeot is Stellantis's largest European brand and already carries the dealer coverage a large car requires — the cost DS was never able to absorb.

The open question is destination. The announcement describes the Peugeot models as intended for "the Chinese market and export to global markets"; Europe is not specified. A Wuhan-built battery-electric Peugeot imported into the EU would fall under the countervailing duties on Chinese-made electric vehicles, which apply by country of origin rather than by brand and which already cover the Shanghai-built Tesla Model 3, the Dacia Spring and BMW's Chinese-built iX3. European type approval, rather than product announcements, will indicate whether these models are intended for the region.

Renault: no equivalent route

Renault has no Chinese joint venture, having exited the market in 2020, no North American presence, and no substantial volume base outside Europe. A clean-sheet large Renault would amortise on European volume alone — the structure of the Safrane and the Vel Satis, neither of which reached scale.

Two routes remain open.

Licensed architecture. Geely holds 34% of Renault Korea, 26.4% of the Brazilian operation and approximately 45% of Horse Powertrain, and through Volvo, Polestar and Zeekr controls large-vehicle architectures — SPA and SEA — that Renault did not fund. Renault Korea at Busan already assembles restyled Geely product for export and is the only plant in the group with a large-vehicle line in operation. Sourcing a large Renault from Busan on Geely architecture would be capital-light and reversible, and consistent with the asset-level agreements both parties have already signed three times.

Concentration below D. Renault's C-to-D range is competitive — Austral 57,181, Scénic 30,420, Rafale 22,264, Espace 17,448 — and D grew 10.4% in 2025. Declining to enter E and funding the D position instead is a defensible allocation, provided the 4.2% growth rate is understood as its cost.

Alpine is the group's only badge positioned above the mainstream, currently applied to a sports car and a D-segment SUV. A licensed architecture under an Alpine badge would be the lowest-cost route to an E-segment presence available to the group.

The Premium Segment Stopped Paying

Europe's E-segment grew 8.5% in 2025 and Germany's grew 37%. In the same year the four companies built to serve it earned mainstream margins, and the most exclusive of them earned the worst. The volume was there. They could not convert it.

The scoping problem, stated first

A piece about European D-segment profitability runs immediately into a disclosure limit that most writing on this subject quietly ignores: none of these companies report margin by segment, and none report margin by region.

BMW's 5.3% automotive EBIT margin is a global figure covering every car it sells everywhere. Mercedes' adjusted return on sales includes China, where it has been discounting heavily, and the United States, where tariffs cost it roughly 1.1 percentage points. Neither number tells you what a 5-Series earns in Germany.

So this piece does not claim to measure European D-segment profitability directly. It claims something narrower and defensible: the four groups whose business is overwhelmingly D-segment-and-above have converged on margins indistinguishable from mass-market carmakers, and the convergence is steepest at the top. That is a statement about companies, not about segments, and the data supports it.

The alternative — dividing group revenue by group units to derive an average selling price — is worse than useless here, and it is worth saying why, because it is the most common error in this analysis. BMW's €133.5bn includes motorcycles and a financial services business. Mercedes' €132.2bn includes vans and mobility. Porsche's automotive revenue is separable; the others' are not on a comparable basis. A revenue-per-unit figure built across those four would be comparing four different definitions and calling the difference an insight.

Build from what is disclosed on a comparable basis, or do not build.

Three readings, in ascending order of how much work they do.

The shallow reading is tariffs. US import tariffs hit all four. BMW quantifies the effect at roughly 1.25 percentage points on automotive EBIT margin; Mercedes' own disclosure implies about 1.1 points, since it reports 5.0% including tariffs and 6.1% excluding them. This is real, it is external, and it is not evidence of anything structural.

The middle reading is one-offs. Porsche's 0.3% is not an operating result. It includes a €3.9bn writedown from reversing its electrification strategy. Strip that out and automotive EBITDA margin was 13.3% against 22.7% the year before — still a severe deterioration, but a different kind of number, and anyone quoting 0.3% as though it described trading performance is misreading the accounts. The honest version of the Porsche story is 22.7% to 13.3%, with a separate €3.9bn admission that a strategic bet failed.

The reading that survives both adjustments is the one this piece is about. Take out tariffs. Take out the writedown. BMW is still at roughly 6.5%, Mercedes at 6.1%, Audi in the 4–6% band it guided to after cutting guidance twice, Porsche at 13.3% and falling. Those are not the numbers of an industry sitting on the profit pool. They are ordinary.

The comparison that does the work

Hold the outcome constant and vary the strategy.

BMW and Mercedes-Benz finished 2025 with revenue within one percent of each other — €133.5bn against €132.2bn — and automotive margins within 0.3 points of each other. On the two measures that matter most, they are the same company.

They got there by opposite routes.

Mercedes has spent four years executing an explicit up-market strategy: deliberately compressing the Entry tier, concentrating investment in Top-End, accepting volume loss in exchange for mix. The logic was that a smaller number of more expensive cars would earn more than a larger number of cheaper ones.

In 2025, Top-End was 268,026 units — 15% of sales, down 5%. The S-Class, the single clearest expression of the entire thesis, fell 17% to 105,564 units. Core fell 10%. Entry fell 10%. The tier the strategy was built to grow shrank, and it shrank slightly less than the tiers it was built to de-emphasise, which is a much weaker result than the strategy required.

BMW made no equivalent bet. It kept breadth, kept volume, and delivered 2,463,681 vehicles — roughly 260,000 more than Mercedes — for the same revenue and a marginally better margin.

Selling 260,000 more cars was worth exactly as much as four years of mix engineering. That is the finding, and it is falsifiable: if Mercedes' 2026 or 2027 results show Top-End growing while margin separates from BMW's, the strategy was merely early and this reading is wrong.

Why the badge stopped protecting the margin

The argument this section will make, once the model-level data supports it:

The premium badge was never a pricing mechanism on its own. It was a claim about hardware the mainstream could not match — engine refinement, chassis engineering, NVH, transmission calibration. Those are expensive to develop, they compound over decades, and they are exactly what an electric powertrain makes free.

This is the same mechanism identified in the NEXA analysis, running in the opposite direction. There, the argument was that electrification lets a company without premium engineering buy its way into premium feel — interior, software, ADAS, acoustics are procurable in a way that a refined six-cylinder is not. The corollary was left implicit and belongs here: if the moat is procurable, the incumbent's thirty-year investment in it stops being a moat.

Maruti's problem is that it has the volume and cannot get the badge. Mercedes' problem is that it has the badge and is discovering what the badge is now worth. They are the same argument observed from opposite ends of the gradient, and neither end is safe.

Volkswagen in America — review in progress

Working brief, not a draft. Evidence assembled, thesis candidates ranked, gaps listed. The strongest reading so far: after fifty years of failing to sell Volkswagens in America, VW's answer is to stop selling Volkswagens.

The candidate thesis

VW has attempted the United States four times on four different strategies, and is now on its fifth. The pattern is not that each attempt failed for its own reasons; it is that the company keeps changing the product and never changes the conclusion.

It is specific to the volume brand and the near-premium brand — the two that compete where price matters and where a 15% tariff is not absorbable.

Audi is the acute case, and worse than "no US plant"

The received framing is that Audi lacks American manufacturing while BMW has Spartanburg and Mercedes has Tuscaloosa. The real position is sharper.

  • The Q5 is Audi's US best-seller and is built in exactly one plant in the world — San José Chiapa, Puebla, opened 2016, capacity above 124,000 units, output overwhelmingly exported to the US and Europe.
  • That plant achieves 63% regional value content, below the USMCA threshold, so the Q5 has faced a 27.5% US tariff since April 2025 rather than duty-free entry. Exports were suspended at one point.
  • EU-built Audis face the 15% European vehicle tariff. US levies cost Volkswagen Group €2.1bn in the first nine months of 2025.
  • The proposed US Audi plant — floated 2023, first as a South Carolina standalone, then as a twin plant at Chattanooga, around $4bn — was halted in January 2026, with Blume saying it will not proceed absent tariff relief and local incentives that have not materialised.
  • The fallback under discussion is Audi building inside Scout's Blythewood plant.

So Audi's route to American localisation now runs through a brand that has not yet built a car, at a plant that does not yet operate, on an architecture supplied by a company VW paid to license it from.

Scout: the bet, and the self-inflicted risk

The bet. $2bn plant at Blythewood, South Carolina. Terra pickup and Traveler SUV, concepts October 2024, production 2027. Over 160,000 reservations at $100 refundable — and 87% of those reservations specify the extended-range EREV rather than the pure battery version.

That 87% is the most interesting number in the file, and it rhymes with a finding already in this body of work: in the European F-segment in 2025, electric flagships fell 36–70% while combustion models in the same price band grew. In America's truck market, given a free choice at no extra cost, buyers picked the option with an engine in it. VW's fifth attempt is succeeding at the reservation stage partly because it is the least electrically radical thing the group has offered.

The risk is distribution, and VW created it. Scout sells direct. NADA sued in February 2025; dealer associations in California and Florida followed; a class action was filed on 3 March 2026 in the Eastern District of Virginia by VW franchise dealers, alleging VW breached its dealer agreements and used Scout as, in effect, a distribution shell. Claimed damages in one action run to $15m and counting.

Tesla and Rivian could go direct because they had no franchise network to breach. VW has one, and it is suing. This is the part of the plan that is not about product at all, and it is the part with the least evidence behind it.

What this atlas is for. Count the D-segment saloons and estates offered by European volume brands and the number is close to zero — Ford, Renault, Opel, Mazda and Honda have all withdrawn, and Stellantis is down to two end-of-life candidates. The mainstream did not lose the segment; it left. The premium brands inherited an uncontested field, and in 2025 they earned roughly 5% on it while Obere Mittelklasse grew 37.3% in Germany.

Volumes. EU 2025 registrations from the EEA CO2 monitoring census under Regulation (EU) 2019/631 — a legally mandated record of every new car registered in the EU, 10,833,597 rows. Commercial names are filed variant-level and inconsistently, so each nameplate is matched by per-brand pattern and then checked against KBA FZ 11 for the German slice. A green tick means two independent official sources agree within 5%; a red figure means they do not and the number should not be used. A dash means Germany has no separate KBA row, so no independent check exists — those figures come from the same pipeline that was 25% wrong on the E-Class until validation caught it, and should be treated as unverified rather than as data.

Assembly region. Where the European-market car is built, not where the brand is from — a BMW X5 is NA, a Cupra Tavascan and a Tesla Model 3 are CN, a Hyundai Tucson and a Kia Sportage are EU. This is not in the census: the EEA Mh field holds the type-approval holder, so an X5 reads “BMW GMBH” despite coming from Spartanburg and a RAV4 reads “Toyota Motor Europe” despite shipping from Japan. These assignments are therefore unsourced, unlike the volumes beside them. Multi-plant models (EX90, Polestar 3, Highlander, CR-V) are assigned their dominant European source and are the likeliest to be wrong.

Before publishing anything from this: every EOL flag needs a live configurator check, Audi's post-2024 nomenclature is unreliable, Volvo saloon availability varies by market, and Chinese brand availability changes quarterly. Verification route is free — KBA Modellreihen tables for official German volumes, manufacturer configurators for what is currently orderable.
