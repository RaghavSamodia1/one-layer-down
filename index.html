One Layer Down

Automotive market analysis · Raghav Samodia · mid-2026
Every European volume figure here comes from the EEA CO2 monitoring census under Regulation (EU) 2019/631 — the statutory record of all 10,833,597 cars registered in the EU in 2025 — normalised to nameplates and cross-checked against Kraftfahrt-Bundesamt registrations for the German slice. 71 of 77 comparable nameplates reconcile within ±5%; the six that do not are flagged rather than reconciled away. Where a piece rests on secondary sources instead, its methodology note says so.

Each piece separates what is reported from what is derived from what is judgement, and closes with the signal that would show it to be wrong. The atlas behind them carries all 171 D-segment-and-above nameplates on sale.

Europe Moved Up. Almost Nobody Profited.

The D and E segments absorbed all of Europe's 2025 growth while every other part of the market shrank. The four groups that own those segments earned mainstream margins. Chinese entrants took most of the increase. Stellantis sold 964 cars above the D-segment and Renault sold none.

The European car market grew by 53,916 units in 2025. The D and E segments grew by 192,798.

Every other part of the market contracted. D and E rose from 18.0% of EU registrations to 19.7% in a single year — D growing 10.4%, E growing 8.5%.

That is the entire net growth of the European car market, concentrated in the segments where European manufacturers are least evenly represented. What follows is what happened to the companies on either side of that line.

The shift is visible inside a single manufacturer

BMW is the clearest illustration, because the movement happened within one range in one year.

The 3 Series was BMW's best-selling European model in 2024 and its fourth in 2025. The two lines cross almost exactly — a loss of 14,046 against a gain of 14,320. Both figures reconcile against German registration data to within 1%, so the crossover is not a classification artefact.

The same shape runs through the rest of the brand: BMW's D-segment volume fell 6.5%, its E-segment volume rose 9.8%, and its F-segment fell 15.1%. The market pattern, reproduced inside one manufacturer's line-up.

One complication worth keeping. BMW's sub-D models — the 1 and 2 Series, X1 and X2 — grew 10%, in a band that contracted 1.6% market-wide. The brand gained share at both ends of its range and lost it only in the middle. That is a more awkward result than a clean premiumisation story, and it is the honest one.

Within saloons the split is almost perfectly by powertrain. Electric flagships collapsed — Tesla Model S −70%, Model X −64%, Audi e-tron GT −42%, Mercedes EQS −36% — while combustion models in the same price band grew: Lamborghini Urus +239%, Revuelto +96%, Bentley Continental GT +33%, Rolls-Royce Cullinan +23%, Mercedes G-Class +16%, Porsche Panamera +5%.

Luxury demand did not fall uniformly. Electric luxury demand fell, and the mechanical end of the market grew. Above roughly €100,000 the buyer is purchasing the thing that cannot be procured, which is precisely what an electric powertrain removes.

The prize is therefore the E-segment and the top of D, not the segment above them.

The companies that own those segments earned ordinary margins

Here a disclosure limit has to be stated before the numbers, because most writing on this subject ignores it: none of these companies report margin by segment, and none report margin by region. BMW's automotive EBIT margin covers every car it sells everywhere; Mercedes' return on sales includes Chinese discounting and US tariffs. Neither tells you what a 5-Series earns in Germany.

So the claim here is narrower than "the D-segment stopped paying". It is that the four groups whose business is overwhelmingly D-and-above have converged on margins indistinguishable from mass-market carmakers, and that the convergence is steepest at the top.

Tariffs are the shallow one. US import duties hit all four. BMW quantifies the effect at roughly 1.25 percentage points; Mercedes' own disclosure implies about 1.1, since it reports 5.0% including tariffs and 6.1% excluding them. Real, external, and not evidence of anything structural.

One-offs are the middle one. Porsche's 0.3% is not a trading result — it includes a €3.9bn writedown from reversing its electrification strategy. Strip it out and automotive EBITDA margin was 13.3% against 22.7% the year before. Anyone quoting 0.3% as operating performance is misreading the accounts; the honest version is 22.7% to 13.3%, plus a separate admission that a strategic bet failed.

What survives both adjustments is the point. Take out tariffs and the writedown: BMW is at roughly 6.5%, Mercedes 6.1%, Audi in the 4–6% band it guided to after cutting guidance twice, Porsche 13.3% and falling. Those are not the numbers of an industry sitting on a profit pool.

A note on what cannot be done with this data. Dividing group revenue by group units to derive an average selling price is the most common error in this analysis and it produces nothing: BMW's €133.5bn includes motorcycles and financial services, Mercedes' €132.2bn includes vans and mobility, and Porsche's automotive revenue is separable where the others' are not. Four different definitions, and the spread between them would look like a finding.

Mix engineering bought Mercedes nothing

BMW and Mercedes finished 2025 with revenue within one percent of each other — €133.5bn against €132.2bn — and automotive margins within 0.3 points. On the two measures that matter, they are the same company. They arrived by opposite routes.

Mercedes spent four years on an explicit up-market strategy: compressing the Entry tier, concentrating investment in Top-End, accepting volume loss for mix. In 2025 Top-End was 268,026 units, 15% of sales, down 5%. The S-Class — the clearest expression of the whole thesis — fell 17% to 105,564. Core fell 10%. Entry fell 10%. The tier the strategy was built to grow shrank, and only marginally less than the tiers it was built to de-emphasise.

BMW made no equivalent bet, kept breadth, and delivered 2,463,681 vehicles — roughly 260,000 more — for the same revenue and a marginally better margin.

Selling 260,000 more cars was worth as much as four years of mix engineering. That is falsifiable: if Mercedes' 2026 or 2027 results show Top-End growing while margin separates from BMW's, the strategy was early rather than wrong.

Chinese manufacturers added 141,463 units of D-and-above volume in twelve months, against a segment complex that grew by roughly 193,000 in total. The entrant group accounts for the majority of the increase.

That is the fuller answer to why a growing segment produced falling margins. The volume arrived; a large share of it went to manufacturers who were not there the year before; and the incumbents held price against them.

Against segment growth of 9.9%, Stellantis went backwards and Renault grew at less than half the rate. Both lost share in the only expanding part of the market.

Two of Europe's three largest makers are not in it at all

Above the D-segment specifically, Stellantis — fourteen brands — sold 964 cars in 2025: a Jeep Grand Cherokee, a Maserati GranTurismo, an MC20. Renault Group sold none; its largest vehicle of any kind is the Rafale, a D-segment SUV. Three German groups took 84% of E and F volume.

The standing objection is that only the German premium marques can charge E-segment money. The Volkswagen ID.7 sold 66,766 units in Europe in 2025 — more than the Mercedes E-Class at 42,483, behind only the 5-Series — and Volkswagen's entry model is a Polo. A mainstream European badge can hold the segment at €50–60,000. The question is not whether it is possible.

Stellantis has moved, to Wuhan. In May 2026 it signed a second joint venture with Dongfeng: two Peugeot electric models from 2027 based on the Concept 6 large saloon and Concept 8 large SUV, plus two Jeeps, in a roughly €1 billion programme of which Stellantis funds €130 million. That is the only route to the volume base the segment requires — a European-only programme amortises against 504,623 registrations shared across some forty nameplates — and at 13% of the cost it is an option rather than a bet.

The open question is destination. The announcement describes the output as intended for "the Chinese market and export to global markets"; Europe is not named. A Wuhan-built Peugeot BEV entering the EU faces countervailing duties applied by origin rather than badge, which already caught the Shanghai Model 3, the Dacia Spring and BMW's Chinese-built iX3. Watch for European type approval, not press releases.

Renault cannot copy it. No Chinese joint venture since 2020, no US presence, no volume base outside Europe. A clean-sheet large Renault funded on European volume alone is the option guaranteed to fail, and it has been tried twice — the Safrane and the Vel Satis.

Two routes remain. Rent the architecture: Geely holds 34% of Renault Korea, 26.4% of the Brazilian operation and roughly 45% of Horse Powertrain, and through Volvo, Polestar and Zeekr owns large-vehicle platforms Renault did not fund. Busan already builds restyled Geely product and is the one plant in the group with a large-vehicle line running. Or concede the segment and fund the C-to-D position, which is genuinely strong — Austral 57,181, Scénic 30,420, Rafale 22,264 — with the 4.2% growth rate understood as the cost.

Alpine is the unspent asset: the only badge in the group with headroom above the mainstream, currently applied to a sports car and a D-segment SUV.

Indicators

Whether Mercedes' Top-End tier grows in 2026. One down year is a market; two is a verdict on the mix strategy.

Whether the Wuhan Peugeots are type-approved for Europe. Decisive, answerable before 2027, and visible in filings rather than announcements.

Where the Concept 6 production model is priced. Below €60,000 it competes where the ID.7 has shown a mainstream badge works. Above €75,000 it is aimed at the band that fell 20.7%.

Whether Chinese D-and-above growth holds near 256%. At that rate the entrant group passes Stellantis's entire large-car business inside two years.

Whether Renault's answer arrives as a supply agreement rather than a programme. An architecture licence announced ahead of any product reveal is the asset-level route working.

Whether the ID.7 holds above 66,000. Much of the argument that this segment is enterable without a premium badge rests on it. A launch-year peak and a run rate look identical in one year of data.

Renault Ended the Alliance and Kept Europe

Part one of four. The equity partnership with Nissan is being dismantled on a published schedule. The replacement arrangements with Geely are structured on entirely different principles — and not one of them touches the European vehicle business, which Renault has funded and kept alone. That is the decision the rest of this series follows from.

Renault is usually described as a company recovering from its Nissan dependency, or as one that has swapped a Japanese partner for a Chinese one. Both readings miss what the documents actually show.

Renault dismantled an equity alliance, built a set of asset-level partnerships in its place, and drew a line around the part of the business it would not partner on at all. The line is the interesting part, and it is where this piece ends.

The 2025 amendment gets the least attention and says the most. Lowering the floor both parties must hold, from 15% to 10%, is not a technical adjustment — it is both sides buying the option to hold less. Neither has to exercise it. Both wanted it available.

What the residual equity buys operationally is nothing: no board control, no product veto, no joint planning obligation. It is a financial position in a company Renault also trades with, and those two facts have been separated.

This is the fact the "Renault has moved on to Geely" reading cannot accommodate. The company's growing European business is the part with no partner in it.

Where the partnerships stop

The Geely arrangements are read as Renault trading one dependency for another. The geography says otherwise.

Korea and Brazil are peripheral markets: real plants, not the business. Horse is the more interesting case because it does touch European operations — it is the combustion and hybrid organisation extracted from Renault itself, with European plants inside it. But that is the point of it. Renault moved the technology it is exiting off its own balance sheet into a venture where a partner funds half the decline.

What none of them touch:

  • The European vehicle plants — Douai, Maubeuge, Sandouville, Flins, Palencia, Valladolid, Tangier, Bursa — wholly Renault's.
  • Ampere, the electric-vehicle subsidiary, wholly Renault's. The R5, the Twingo and the architecture beneath them were funded alone.
  • CMF-B and CMF-C/D, which carry the volume range, are Renault's to license out rather than something it licenses in.

The Common Module Family was jointly funded with Nissan from 2013 and extended to Mitsubishi after 2016 — genuinely shared engineering, and the alliance's real achievement. Its successors are not shared. AmpR Small, the architecture under the R5 and the new Micra, was developed by Ampere and is sold to Nissan rather than co-funded with it.

So Renault partnered on peripheral geographies and on declining technology, and funded the European core and the electric future by itself. That is a deliberate line and the most important thing about its current position.

The replacement is a different instrument

The Nissan alliance was cross-shareholdings between parent companies — an attempt to align two whole firms through equity, which produced two decades of governance friction and one criminal case. The Geely arrangements have no parent-level equity at all. They are stakes in assets:

Horse is the clearest illustration: Renault and Geely at roughly 45% each, Saudi Aramco at 10%, the venture valued at €7.4 billion. Geely did not buy Renault's engine business — the two co-created a merchant supplier out of it, Geely gaining powertrain engineering it can sell to third parties, Renault removing a declining capital burden from its accounts.

Nobody owns anybody. Each stake is separately negotiated, separately valued and separately exitable.

Geely stopped buying carmakers in 2019

The instrument is not specific to Renault, and its history explains the change.

Geely's acquisition era ran 2006 to 2019: Volvo Cars from Ford for $1.8 billion in 2010, a 9.7% Daimler stake in 2018, Proton and Lotus in 2017. As a portfolio it bought engineering capability, brand credibility and market access. As a balance sheet it also bought pension liabilities, legacy plants, union agreements and an obligation to keep marginal businesses alive.

Since 2022 the pattern has been consistent: a minority stake in a specific asset, product brought to fill its unused capacity, ownership and liabilities left with the incumbent. Renault Korea, Renault do Brasil and Horse all fit. So does the Ford joint venture at Almussafes announced in July 2026 — 34% for around €221 million, valuing the plant at roughly €650 million, giving Geely its first European manufacturing base without a brand changing hands.

Sixteen years ago Geely bought Volvo Cars from Ford for $1.8 billion. In 2026 it took a third of Ford's Spanish factory for €221 million. Same counterparty, one-eighth the price, comparable European industrial access. What changed is the definition of the valuable asset: in 2010 it was a brand, in 2026 a building with the right postcode.

An estimate, and what it is worth

Everything in this section is the author's own modelling. It is not reported, not disclosed, and should be weighted well below the figures above.

Renault's supply arrangements with Nissan and Mitsubishi generate revenue that is not separately disclosed. A first attempt to size it produced roughly €1.5bn for 2025 and €2.5bn for 2030, built from visible headline programmes. That understated it, because it omitted three asset classes:

  1. Nissan's European light commercial twins — Interstar, Primastar, Townstar. Individually unglamorous enough to be forgotten in a top-down estimate; collectively substantial and stable, because LCV cycles are long.
  2. The Micra at Douai — a full passenger programme treated as a badging exercise rather than a revenue line.
  3. Mitsubishi's European platform twins — Colt, ASX and Grandis. An entire brand's European presence supplied from Renault architecture, sitting outside the Nissan relationship and therefore outside the mental model.

Rebuilt from enumerated assets, the estimates move to roughly €3bn (2025) and €5.5bn (2030).

The general lesson is more reliable than the numbers: revenue projections built top-down from strategic narrative systematically understate, because narrative remembers flagship programmes and forgets vans. Build from enumerated assets, plant by plant and nameplate by nameplate, or do not build.

A crossover date follows from this model — the point where Renault's Geely-linked business exceeds its Nissan-linked one, around 2028–29. Earlier drafts of this analysis led with that date. It should not lead, because it rests entirely on the estimates above rather than on anything disclosed, and a single unreported assumption moves it by years. It is a directional statement about which relationship is growing, not a forecast.

What Renault becomes

Judgement, from the reported material above.

Renault owns plants — Chennai, Douai, and parts of Busan and Paraná. It owns architectures — CMF-B, CMF-C/D, AmpR Small, and through Horse a share of a powertrain portfolio. It increasingly monetises them by supplying other people's brands, while renting in capabilities it chose not to fund.

Two consequences, pointing opposite ways.

Favourably: supply revenue is structurally less cyclical and less capital-hungry at the margin than brand revenue. Renault is converting fixed assets from a cost of competing into a source of income, which is the right move for a mid-scale manufacturer that cannot win a global volume war.

Unfavourably: a supplier does not control its customers' roadmaps, and one competing with its own customers is in an unstable position. Renault sells Nissan the architecture for the Micra and competes with the Micra. It builds Geely product in Busan and competes with Geely in Europe.

And the cost of the line it drew round Europe is the subject of part three: a company that funds Europe alone has only European scale to amortise against, which is why it has nothing above the D-segment.

Indicators

The 10% floor. Both sides hold the option to reduce from 15%. Whoever exercises first, and who buys, says more than any joint statement.

Whether Busan or Paraná receives a second Geely product. One restyled model is a capacity deal; two is a platform commitment.

Whether Nissan sources from Renault outside Europe and India. Confined to those regions this is a legacy wind-down being monetised. Extended to ASEAN or South America, Renault has built a merchant business.

Whether a fifth Geely asset deal follows the template. Four is a pattern; five with a new counterparty in a new region makes it the standard instrument.

The disconfirming signal. This piece argues Renault deliberately ring-fenced its European core. If Renault takes a partner into a European vehicle plant, into Ampere, or into its next passenger architecture, that reading is wrong — and the more ordinary explanation, that it partners wherever capital is short, would be right instead.

The Divorced Children: What Remains, and the Stakes

Part two of four. Nothing that survives the lengthy breakup is jointly owned. What is left is a set of separately negotiated supply contracts, each priced on its own, between three companies that still hold each other's shares out of habit.

Part one described the equity alliance as terminal. That raises an obvious question, because Renault, Nissan and Mitsubishi visibly still work together: Nissans are built in Renault factories, Mitsubishis are rebadged Renaults, and the Indian operation runs on shared plant.

The answer is that all of it is now transactional. Every surviving link is a supply agreement with its own terms, its own duration and its own price. Not one of them is a shared asset, a joint programme, or a pooled investment. That distinction is what separates an alliance from a customer relationship, and it is the whole content of what remains.

Europe: three supply contracts, priced separately

Nissan's light commercial range is Renault's. The Townstar, Primastar and Interstar are the Kangoo, Trafic and Master. In the 2025 EU registration data they total 9,182 units against Nissan's 193,407 — under 5% of the European business, because Nissan's European volume is overwhelmingly the Sunderland-built Qashqai and Juke.

Nissan's next small cars are Renault's. The new Micra is built by Ampere at Douai on Renault's small-EV architecture. A Twingo derivative follows — designed by Nissan, engineered and produced by Renault. This is the boundary moving: light commercials were peripheral, small passenger cars are not.

Mitsubishi's European range is largely Renault's already. The Colt is a Clio, the ASX a Captur, the Grandis a Symbioz. In 2025 those three were 59% of Mitsubishi's 43,356 EU registrations, up from 46% a year earlier — not because Mitsubishi bought more, but because its own product fell 43% while the borrowed product held.

Three arrangements, three different depths, and no common structure. Nissan buys vans and is starting to buy small cars. Mitsubishi buys most of a range. Each was negotiated on its own terms.

The Common Module Family was the alliance's genuine achievement — a modular architecture initiated in 2013, extended to Mitsubishi after 2016, and jointly funded. Nissan contributed to it and has a claim on it. That is why the Qashqai and Juke can be simultaneously Nissan's own products and alliance engineering without contradiction.

The evolution is that the joint category is not being replaced. CMF's successors are not co-funded. AmpR Small — the architecture under the new Micra — was developed by Ampere, Renault's EV subsidiary, and Nissan buys access to it. The Twingo derivative is the same. Mitsubishi's European models were never co-developed at all.

So the trend across the table is one-directional: co-development at the top, rebadging at the bottom, and every new arrangement entering at the bottom. A company on a platform it helped design has a claim on the next generation. A company buying finished vehicles has a purchase order.

India: the cleanest case, because the ownership actually moved

India is where the transactional logic is most explicit, because an asset changed hands rather than being shared.

Renault acquired Nissan's 51% of Renault Nissan Automotive India Private Limited and took full ownership of the Chennai plant from August 2025 — a site with over 400,000 units of capacity that has built more than 2.8 million vehicles since 2010.

What Nissan kept: roughly 300 dealer outlets, forty years of Indian brand equity, an export programme, and a capacity reservation at a factory it no longer owns.

What Nissan gave up: any say in what the factory builds.

The evidence that this is settled rather than transitional is the product. Nissan's locally built Indian range is the Gravite — a Renault Triber — and the Tekton, a Duster on CMF-B. Every Nissan built in India is a Renault underneath.

That is not a partnership dissolving messily. It is a partnership that has finished dissolving and been replaced by a purchase order.

What each party is actually holding

Renault holds the assets. Chennai outright. Douai, and the small-EV architecture the Micra and Twingo derivatives run on. CMF-B. The European light-commercial range that Nissan sells under its own badge. Renault emerged from the breakup owning the industrial layer the other two now buy from.

Nissan holds a brand and a network, and one genuine manufacturing base. Sunderland, the Qashqai and the Juke are Nissan's own and account for 77% of its European volume. In India it holds distribution without production. The position is coherent where it owns product and thin where it does not.

Mitsubishi holds the least. A 42,000-unit European business that is majority someone else's product and falling, with its own range down 43% in a year. Its real business is elsewhere — ASEAN at 256,000 units of its 797,000 global total — and the European arrangement is a small operation kept alive by supply rather than a strategy.

Why "alliance" is now the wrong word

An alliance implies pooled risk. These arrangements pool nothing.

If the Micra fails at Douai, Renault carries the plant and Nissan carries the marketing. If Mitsubishi's Colt stops selling, Renault loses a customer and Mitsubishi loses a model. If Chennai runs below capacity, that is Renault's problem alone — Nissan reserved capacity, it did not buy any.

Each party's downside is bounded by its own contract. That is a normal commercial relationship between manufacturers, and it is what the alliance has become: three companies that trade with each other, hold each other's shares for reasons that predate the current arrangement, and have no joint obligation beyond the specific agreements signed.

The lengthy breakup did not end the cooperation. It ended the shared exposure, which is the only part that made it an alliance.

What Next for Renault: Time to Go Big in Europe

Part three of four. The only growing part of the European market is the part Renault is not in. It has no route to build its way there, one realistic route to rent its way there, and an unspent badge. The piece closes with a transaction nobody has proposed.

The lengthy breakup left Renault owning the industrial layer: Chennai outright, Douai and the small-EV architecture, CMF-B, and a share of the powertrain business it extracted into Horse. Parts one and two set out how that happened and what it left the other two partners holding.

This part asks the harder question. Renault emerged from the breakup as the party with the assets. What should it do with them?

The entire net growth of the European market in 2025 came from two segments. Every other part of it contracted.

Renault Group's D-and-above volume grew 4.2% against segment growth of 9.9% — less than half the rate, which is a loss of share in the only expanding part of the market. Above the D-segment it sold nothing at all. Its largest vehicle of any kind is the Rafale, a D-segment SUV at 22,264 units.

So the position is not that Renault is under-represented in a premium niche. It is absent from the only growing part of its home market while new entrants take it.

What Renault cannot do

It cannot build its way there. A clean-sheet large Renault would have to amortise against European volume alone: 504,623 E-segment registrations shared across roughly forty nameplates. Renault has no Chinese joint venture, having exited in 2020, no US presence, and no volume base outside Europe of consequence.

This has been attempted twice. The Safrane and the Vel Satis were both funded on European volume and neither reached scale. The arithmetic has not improved since — it has worsened, because the segment now contains Chinese entrants pricing against a domestic cost base.

And it should not aim at the top of the market. The F-segment fell 12.1% in 2025, with saloons down 20.7%. Within saloons the decline is almost perfectly a powertrain story: Tesla Model S −70%, Model X −64%, Audi e-tron GT −42%, Mercedes EQS −36%, while combustion models in the same band grew — Urus +239%, Continental GT +33%, Cullinan +23%, Panamera +5%. Above roughly €100,000 the buyer is purchasing something that cannot be procured, which is exactly what an electric powertrain removes. No volume manufacturer can compete there.

The target is the E-segment, which grew 8.5%, and the top of D, which grew 10.4%.

What the ID.7 proves

The standing objection is that only the German premium marques can charge E-segment money.

The Volkswagen ID.7 registered 66,766 units in Europe in 2025 — more than the Mercedes E-Class at 42,483, behind only the BMW 5-Series. Volkswagen's entry model in Europe is a Polo.

A mainstream European badge can hold the E-segment at €50–60,000. That is settled as a matter of fact, by a direct competitor, last year. The question for Renault is not whether it is possible.

What Renault can do

Rent the architecture. The counterparty is already under contract. Geely holds 34% of Renault Korea, 26.4% of the Brazilian operation and roughly 45% of Horse Powertrain, and through Volvo, Polestar and Zeekr it owns large-vehicle platforms — SPA and SEA — that Renault did not fund.

The specific architectures matter here, because Renault's own pipeline stops at the wrong size. Its funded platforms are CMF-B, CMF-C/D and AmpR Small — supermini to D-segment. It has nothing above that and no programme to build one.

Wishful thinking: the transaction nobody has proposed

What follows is speculation, clearly marked as such. It is not a forecast and nothing in the reporting suggests it is under discussion. It is included because the assets involved fit together unusually well, and because the alternative currently being pursued looks worse.

Three facts from this series sit adjacent to each other.

Renault holds dead equity. 15% of Nissan directly, plus a residual position in a French trust being sold down, with the required floor lowered to 10% in March 2025. It confers no control, no product access and no board influence. It is a financial holding in a company Renault also sells to.

Infiniti has retail capacity it cannot currently fill. Nissan's premium brand delivered 153,415 US vehicles in 2017 and 58,070 in 2024, pacing near 50,000 in 2025. Published dealer counts range from 191 to 342 and are unreliable, but the ratio is not: throughput per outlet is down roughly 67% whatever the denominator.

This is the fact that has moved against the argument, and it should be stated before the argument rather than after it. Nissan has committed to five new Infiniti models over five years — the QX65 in 2026, then a midsize hybrid SUV, a performance V6 sedan and two large hybrid SUVs. That is four additions on top of the three surviving nameplates, and it is a company refilling a network rather than abandoning one.

Alpine needs American retail and has none. It has a confirmed 2027 US entry, a seven-model plan, and a US-relevant European track record of 2,236 cars once the A290 is excluded.

The transaction: Renault exchanges part of its Nissan holding for a position in Infiniti — equity, a distribution agreement, or both — and enters North America through the Infiniti network.

What each party would get. Renault converts a stake that does nothing into US retail access it cannot otherwise buy at its scale, and gives Alpine a network on day one rather than building one for a two-thousand-unit brand. Nissan converts an underused premium network into a partner-funded one, reduces a cross-shareholding both sides have already agreed to shrink, and keeps Infiniti alive without funding a full premium programme alone. Infiniti dealers get product in a showroom running at a third of its scaled volume.

Why it will probably not happen, stated as plainly as the case for it:

  • Alpine is reportedly going elsewhere. It is described as in talks with AutoNation for an independent US retail presence rather than alliance channels. If that is right, Renault has already considered the alliance route and rejected it — which is itself the most interesting fact in this piece, because it means alliance membership no longer confers a retail advantage over what can be bought on the open market.
  • Brand adjacency cuts the wrong way. An Alpine counter inside a Nissan store that also houses Infiniti is three brands deep and premium in none of them.
  • Neither party wants deeper entanglement. The whole direction of the breakup has been toward arm's-length contracts. A joint retail venture reverses it.
  • Nissan is filling the showrooms itself. Five models in five years is capital committed. A network with product coming is not spare capacity, and the window in which this transaction made obvious sense may have closed while it was being described.
  • Alpine has already changed its US product twice — from mid-size and large electric crossovers to a next-generation A110 with a combustion option, reportedly because of tariffs and the reduction of US EV incentives. A company revising its product and powertrain is not in a position to also revise its distribution model.

The case for taking it seriously anyway is narrower than it was a year ago, and it is this: Renault's alternative is still to fund an American retail network from scratch for a brand selling roughly two thousand relevant cars a year, while holding a Nissan stake it has agreed to reduce and cannot otherwise deploy. A shared showroom with a brand that now has five models coming is a different proposition from a rescue of an empty one — arguably a better one for Alpine, since the traffic returns — but it is no longer a transaction where one side is obviously desperate. That was the version that would have been cheap.

Indicators

Whether the AutoNation arrangement is confirmed or lapses. It is reported rather than announced. A signed agreement settles how Alpine reaches American buyers; a return to Nissan channels would indicate the alliance retains more operational value than the current direction suggests.

Whether either party moves below the 10% floor. The option exists on both sides. Whoever exercises it, and who buys, determines whether any equity-for-access transaction is even available.

Whether Renault's European large-car answer arrives as a supply agreement rather than a programme. An architecture licence — most plausibly Geely-sourced — announced ahead of any product reveal is the asset-level route working. An internally funded flagship is the Vel Satis for a third time.

Whether Alpine is given a product above €60,000. It is the only badge in the group that could carry one, and it is currently being spent on a hot hatch and a D-segment SUV.

Whether Infiniti's two announced electric models arrive and hold the brand above 50,000 US units. If they do, the network is committed and the speculation above is moot. If they do not, the question of what Infiniti's retail is for becomes live for Nissan whether or not Renault is interested.

What Next for Nissan and Mitsubishi

Part four of four. Mitsubishi sells 797,000 vehicles a year and 42,000 of them are in Europe. Nissan sells 3.2 million and lost money doing it. The lengthy breakup has left both with a European supply relationship that matters far less than the markets nobody is writing about.

The first three parts of this series followed the lengthy breakup from Renault's side: an equity partnership in terminal decline, a residue of supply contracts, and a company whose operative relationships are now with Geely. This part asks what the other two are left holding — and the answer is only partly European.

The proportions first

Most commentary on the alliance is written about Europe, because Europe is where the rebadging is visible. For the two Japanese partners it is close to the least important thing they do.

Two different relationships are visible in that table and they are routinely conflated.

Co-developed platform, own body. The Qashqai and Juke are Nissan-designed vehicles on architecture the alliance built together. Nissan contributed to CMF and draws on it. That is what a platform alliance is for, and it is why 77% of Nissan's European volume is simultaneously "Nissan's own product" and "alliance architecture" without contradiction.

Rebadge. The Colt is a Clio. The Townstar is a Kangoo. Same vehicle, different badge, no engineering contribution from the brand selling it.

The evolution is that the first category is converting into the second. CMF was jointly funded; its successors are not. The new Micra runs on AmpR Small — Renault's small-EV architecture, developed by Ampere, which Nissan did not co-fund. The Twingo derivative that follows is the same arrangement. Mitsubishi's three European models were never co-developed at all.

So Nissan's platform dependence is not new. What changed is that it used to be a joint investment and is now a purchase. A company on a platform it helped design has a claim on the next generation. A company buying finished vehicles does not.

That changes at the next cycle rather than this one. The new Micra is built by Ampere at Douai on Renault's small-EV architecture, and a Twingo derivative follows — designed by Nissan, engineered and produced by Renault. Nissan is entering that arrangement deliberately, for architectures it chose not to fund, while keeping the products that work.

The two companies should not be discussed as a single junior bloc, which is the standard treatment. Mitsubishi's dependence is arriving by attrition. Nissan's is being negotiated.

Where the actual problems are

Nissan's difficulty is American and Chinese, not European. US sales fell 14.0% and Chinese sales 11.6% in FY2025, and the company posted an operating loss. Europe is 6% of the business and roughly stable. Any restructuring that begins with the European range is addressing the smallest available number.

Within North America the specific issue is Infiniti, which delivered 153,415 US vehicles in 2017 and 58,070 in 2024, pacing near 50,000 in 2025. Standalone showrooms are folding into Nissan stores, the QX50 and QX55 are discontinued, and throughput per outlet is down roughly two-thirds.

But Nissan is refilling it rather than winding it down, and the product plan is the clearest statement of intent the brand has made in a decade. Five new models in five years: the QX65, a coupe-profile midsize SUV arriving in 2026, followed by a midsize hybrid SUV, a performance V6 sedan and two large hybrid SUVs — four additions on top of the three nameplates that survive the discontinuations.

Two things about that plan are worth noting. It is a commitment of real capital to a brand many observers had written off, which changes what the network is for. And the powertrains have moved: earlier reporting had the renewal built around two electric models, and what has been described since is four hybrids and a combustion sedan. That is the same retreat from electric luxury visible in the European F-segment, where electric flagships fell 36–70% in 2025 while combustion models in the same price band grew.

And in India, Nissan owns no plant at all. It exited the RNAIPL joint venture; Renault took full ownership of Chennai from August 2025. Nissan retains roughly 300 dealer outlets, forty years of brand equity, an export programme and a capacity reservation at a factory it no longer owns. Every Nissan built in India is now a Renault underneath — the Gravite is a Triber, the Tekton a Duster on CMF-B.

The structural weakness is worth naming: a company with one supplier is not a customer, it is a subsidiary with extra steps. Nissan's Indian product cadence, segment coverage and cost position are all set by a firm that competes with it in the same showrooms. If Renault does not build a midsize SUV, Nissan does not sell one.

Nissan management has said it is open to contract manufacturing or collaboration with other Indian manufacturers, with no restrictions on who it would consider, and has separately confirmed the Chennai capacity reservation. Read together, those statements say the Renault arrangement is non-exclusive and Nissan intends to keep it that way.

The obvious second supplier is Stellantis, and specifically Citroën. Stellantis holds three Indian manufacturing positions — Thiruvallur, the CK Birla joint venture building Citroën's C-Cubed range; Hosur, a powertrain plant positioned as a global ICE export hub; and Ranjangaon, the 50:50 Tata joint venture that has built over 1.37 million vehicles. Citroën's Indian retail volumes are a small fraction of what that footprint can absorb.

The asymmetry runs exactly opposite to the Renault one. Stellantis has Indian plants and no volume; Nissan has volume potential and no plants. Citroën's C3, Basalt and C3 Aircross sit on a Smart Car platform configured down to Indian price points — precisely the segment where Nissan's Indian range is thinnest and where Renault has nothing to lend it.

Both parties have done this before. Nissan has badge-engineered Renault product in India for a decade, from the Terrano to the Tekton and Gravite. Stellantis has supplied badge-engineered vehicles to brands it does not own — the Toyota ProAce, the Ram ProMaster City. Neither would be conceding anything it has not already conceded in public.

The tell, if it happens, is a capacity announcement at Thiruvallur or Hosur before any product announcement: utilisation moves first, badges follow. The disconfirming signal is equally clear — if Nissan's next Indian launch is a third Renault derivative, it has accepted single-supplier dependence as the price of capital discipline.

Mitsubishi's difficulty is that its strong region is its least defensible one. ASEAN at 256,000 units is the business, and it is the market Chinese manufacturers are entering hardest — BYD, GWM and others have been building capacity in Thailand and Indonesia specifically. Mitsubishi withdrew from China in 2023 after being outcompeted there; the same competitors are now arriving in the markets it retreated to.

North America at 165,000 units is its second region and depends on a small range in a market where its brand equity is thin.

What each should do

Mitsubishi should formalise Europe rather than fund it. At 42,000 units and 59% borrowed, the honest structures are a defined distribution agreement with fixed product and terms, or withdrawal as it withdrew from China. Continuing to fund a shrinking independent range alongside a growing borrowed one is the most expensive available option, and the least likely to produce a European business worth having.

The capital that releases belongs in ASEAN, where Mitsubishi has manufacturing, distribution and forty years of position, and where it is about to be attacked by the companies that removed it from China.

Nissan should resolve Infiniti rather than manage it. The brand is either worth refilling with product — which means committing to a premium programme in a segment it has been losing for eight years — or worth converting into something else. Part three of this series argues that the something else has an obvious counterparty in Alpine, which needs American retail and has none.

And Nissan should be explicit about which architectures it is buying and which it is keeping. Sunderland, the Qashqai and the Juke are real assets. The Micra and Twingo derivatives move the boundary of what Nissan designs. Renting specific architectures it declined to fund is defensible; letting the boundary move because each individual decision is cheaper than the alternative is how a company stops being one. Those two look identical for several years and then do not.

Whether Chinese brands take ASEAN share from Mitsubishi at the rate they took China. This is the consequential number for the company and it is not a European one.

Whether a capacity announcement appears at Thiruvallur or Hosur. It would be the first concrete sign that Nissan has found a second Indian supplier, and it would precede any product reveal.

The Japanese entry into Western markets in the 1970s and 1980s produced exactly this reaction, and exactly the same category of response.

The current defensive structures deserve the same scepticism. Stellantis at 13% of a Dongfeng programme, Leapmotor distributed through Stellantis dealers, the various technology licensing arrangements — these are the joint ventures of the 1980s in contemporary dress, and the historical hit rate on them is close to zero.

Where the difference actually is

Two honest disanalogies, because the parallel is not perfect and pretending otherwise weakens it.

The Japanese never took the Western manufacturers' home market. Chinese brands did. GM's Chinese volume fell from 4.04m to just over 1.8m; Chevrolet's from 641,320 to 52,774. Western manufacturers were never significant in Japan, so there was nothing to lose there. This time the incumbents lost the largest market in the world before the competition arrived in theirs.

And the growth rate is not comparable to the level. Chinese brands at 4.3% is small. But they added 230,976 units in a year, in a market that grew 53,916 in total. The share is a foothold; the trajectory is not. Anyone arguing this is overblown has to hold both of those facts at once, and the honest version of the argument is not yet a crisis rather than not a threat.

What the companies handling it well have in common

They are not doing anything structurally clever. They are doing the thing they were already good at, harder.

Toyota resisted the pure-electric consensus and kept building hybrids while being told it was behind. The 2025 European data settled that argument: electric flagships fell 36–70% while combustion models in the same price band grew, and the F-segment decline was almost entirely a powertrain story. Toyota was not slow. It was right about what customers would buy.

BMW has €45.5bn, has bought nothing in twenty-five years, and is answering the Neue Klasse question with its own engineering. It grew 4.1% in Europe in 2025 against a market that grew 0.5%, and it did it while retaining the six-cylinder engines and rear-drive layouts its customers came for.

Renault kept its European core entirely unpartnered — the plants, Ampere, the small-EV architecture — while partnering on peripheral geographies and the combustion business it is exiting. Its European volume grew 6.1%. The R5 is a retro small car built in France, which is the least technologically radical product strategy available and it is working.

Suzuki never chased any of it, stayed in the segments and markets it understands, and remains one of the most consistently profitable volume manufacturers in the industry.

The common factor is not defensiveness. It is that all four declined to reorganise themselves around a competitor's strategy, and instead did more of what their customers already valued them for.

What to take from it

Do not restructure around the entrant. The 1980s joint ventures were all rational at the time and all dissolved. Structure does not substitute for product.

Do not concede the segments you are good at in order to chase the ones the entrant defined. The manufacturers losing share fastest in Europe are not the ones that ignored Chinese competition; they are the ones that were already weak in their own core.

Do learn the specific things worth learning. Cost engineering, development speed, and supplier integration are genuine Chinese advantages and can be adopted without adopting the strategy. Toyota's Chinese development centre and Honda's Momenta and CATL arrangements are exactly this — importing capability, not restructuring around a rival.

And keep the counter in view. 4.3% with a doubling growth rate is a different problem in three years. The argument here is that the response should be product rather than structure, not that there is nothing to respond to.

Whether the F-segment powertrain split repeats in 2026. One year is a market; two is a preference.

BMW Has €45.5bn and Should Keep Not Spending It

It holds the deepest balance sheet in the industry outside Toyota and has not bought a carmaker in twenty-five years. That looks like scar tissue from Rover. On the record of what acquisitions have actually achieved in this industry, it is the correct policy — and the current menu does not contain an exception.

BMW's automotive net financial assets stood at €45.5 billion in 2025 — more than Volkswagen's Automotive Division, more than Mercedes' industrial business, and more than Ford, General Motors and Stellantis hold between them.

A balance sheet that size invites the question every year. The answer has been no since 2000.

BMW bought a British volume manufacturer, could not fix it, and exited six years later at a cost that pushed the whole group into loss.

But two of the three outcomes were good, and the caricature obscures which two.

MINI was the prize and BMW kept it. It registered 114,673 cars in the EU in 2025, up 16.8% in a market that grew 0.5%.

Rolls-Royce was the better transaction and was barely a transaction. In the 1998 auction Volkswagen bid successfully for Rolls-Royce Motor Cars — the Crewe plant and Bentley — while BMW separately secured the rights to the Rolls-Royce name and badge from the aero-engine company for a fraction of the price. VW got the factory; BMW got the name, built Goodwood, and has run the marque since 2003. It registered 549 European cars in 2025 at extreme margins.

Land Rover was the loss, and it was structural rather than tactical. BMW sold at £1.85bn because it needed to stop the bleeding. Ford sold it on to Tata in 2008, also at a loss. Nobody made money on Land Rover for two decades.

So the record is: one badge acquired almost free that became a permanent franchise, one brand inside a failed deal that became a growth engine, and one asset sold under duress. The thing that cost £1.79bn was buying the volume manufacturer. The things that worked cost almost nothing.

What the record actually establishes

The lesson usually drawn is "BMW should not acquire." The correct and more general one is that acquisition does not solve a product problem, and the industry's own history is unusually clear on this.

The Japanese entry of the 1970s and 80s produced a generation of defensive structures. Not one survives.

Chinese development capability — the reflex to resist

The apparently sophisticated argument runs: BMW's real gap is not a badge but development speed in electric powertrain, cockpit software and driver assistance, in the market setting the pace. Chinese brands took European D-and-above volume from 55,237 to 196,700 in a year. So BMW should buy a Chinese engineering base or a controlling position in a Chinese technology supplier.

This is the 1980s reflex wearing a 2026 suit, and it should be resisted for the same reason.

Look at what the two companies actually handling this well have done. Toyota built — it renamed its largest Chinese R&D base, moved development authority to it, and folded in engineers from its three joint ventures. It did not acquire anything. Honda contracted — its Ye series uses Momenta for driver assistance and CATL for a cell-to-chassis battery platform. Those are supply agreements, not acquisitions.

Neither bought a company. Both got the capability. An acquisition would have added integration risk, political exposure and a balance-sheet commitment to precisely the fastest-moving part of the industry — the part where owning an asset is worth least.

The gap is real. Acquisition is not the instrument that closes it.

What BMW is actually doing, and why it is working

BMW registered 650,232 cars in the EU in 2025, up 4.1% against a market that grew 0.5%. It grew 9.8% in the E-segment and 10% below the D-segment — gaining share at both ends of its range.

It did that while retaining six-cylinder engines and rear-drive layouts, developing the Neue Klasse in Germany, and absorbing roughly 1.25 percentage points of tariff impact on automotive margin without cutting investment.

The €45.5 billion is not idle capital waiting for a deal. It is the reason the tariff hit was absorbable and the reason the Neue Klasse is funded internally rather than through a partner who would own part of the result.

And the market has been telling manufacturers something all year about how much technological leadership buyers are actually paying for. In the European F-segment, saloons fell 20.7% while SUVs fell 4.2% — and within saloons, electric flagships collapsed (Model S −70%, Model X −64%, e-tron GT −42%, EQS −36%) while combustion models in the same price band grew (Urus +239%, Continental GT +33%, Cullinan +23%, G-Class +16%, Panamera +5%).

Above a certain price, buyers chose the mechanical and the familiar over the technically advanced. A company whose answer to competitive pressure is "more of what we are known for, executed harder" is reading that correctly.

The verdict

BMW should not be preparing for a big acquisition, and the reason is not Rover.

The reason is that no acquisition in this industry has solved a product problem for the acquirer. NUMMI did not save GM. Rover–Honda did not save Rover. Daimler–Chrysler was unwound. Stellantis is the largest recent attempt at scale through merger and its industrial net position halved in 2025, to €6.7bn.

What worked for BMW was buying a name for almost nothing and building a business behind it, twice. What cost £1.79bn was buying a company.

Twenty-five years of restraint reads as trauma. Measured against the record, it is the policy the record supports — and the current menu contains no exception to it.

The Global Car Is Over. Development Is Going Local.

Toyota moved its Chinese development to China and put Chinese engineers in charge of it. Honda buys its Chinese autonomy stack and battery platform from Chinese suppliers. Stellantis gave up and licensed an Indian platform. The manufacturers that do not follow will not be global manufacturers — and the traffic is already running in both directions.

For thirty years the economics of the industry rewarded the opposite of this. A platform developed once and amortised across every market was the whole logic of scale, and the companies that did it best — Volkswagen with MQB, Toyota with TNGA — were rewarded for it.

That model is failing in the two largest growth markets simultaneously, and the responses to it are the clearest available signal of which manufacturers understand what has changed.

America proved the model first, and everyone has forgotten

This is not a new problem. It is a solved problem that has come back.

Japanese manufacturers established American design and engineering operations from the 1970s onward — Calty in California, Honda's American R&D in Ohio, Nissan Design in San Diego — for a reason that is now being rediscovered elsewhere: American vehicle preferences could not be served from Tokyo. Full-size pickups, three-row family SUVs and minivans were not scaled-up versions of Japanese products. They were different vehicles for a different use case, and they had to be designed by people who lived with it.

The result was a generation of vehicles that exist because local engineering existed — the Odyssey, the Ridgeline, the Tundra, the Sequoia. None of them would have emerged from a global programme run out of Japan, because none of them made sense as a variant of anything.

The lesson was learned, applied, and then treated as a solved American peculiarity rather than a general principle. It was the general principle.

China is forcing the same lesson, faster and at higher cost

The Chinese market now diverges from global norms on the three things that currently differentiate a car — powertrain, cockpit software, and driver assistance — and it moves faster than a global development cycle can track. Two Japanese manufacturers have responded, differently.

Toyota moved the authority. In August 2023 it renamed its largest Chinese R&D base Intelligent ElectroMobility R&D Center by Toyota — IEM by TOYOTA — and folded in engineers from the R&D operations of its three Chinese joint ventures: FAW Toyota, GAC Toyota and BYD Toyota. The remit is local development of all electrified powertrains, and local design of smart cockpits and driver assistance "that better fit the actual situation in China." Alongside it, an explicit cost programme built on developing a local supplier base and redesigning parts for it.

That is not a satellite office. It is the transfer of product decisions to the market they are for.

Honda went further and bought the stack. Its Ye series is a China-only EV line — six models planned by 2027, built by both its Chinese joint ventures, with the GAC Honda and Dongfeng Honda GT flagships shown at Auto Shanghai in 2025. The significant part is the sourcing. Honda is co-developing its driver assistance with Momenta, a Chinese AI firm, and its battery platform with CATL, including cell-to-chassis construction and LFP cells.

Honda did not localise its own technology for China. It adopted Chinese technology for China. Those are different decisions and the second is the more radical one — it concedes that on the differentiating systems, the local supplier is ahead.

India is the third instance, and Stellantis took the third option

The Indian version of the problem is cost rather than software. A vehicle engineered to European price points cannot be sold profitably at Indian ones, and localising a European architecture far enough to try has repeatedly failed the business case.

Stellantis confronted this directly for its next India-developed global Jeep and rejected both of its own platforms. STLA Medium was judged unviable to localise on cost. Smart Car — the platform Stellantis itself pioneered in India for global affordable cars — is front-wheel-drive only with dated electronics, and Jeep treats all-wheel drive as non-negotiable. A clean-sheet architecture was estimated at one to two billion dollars and five to seven years.

So Stellantis licensed Tata's ARGOS, the platform under the Sierra, with Tata also supplying the 1.5-litre turbo-petrol engine. The vehicle is targeted for 2028 and export to more than fifty markets. Tata licenses the architecture and supplies the engine; Jeep designs the body and interior; Tata has no co-development role and no stake in the product's commercial success.

That is the third response, and it is worth naming precisely because it is not the same as the other two. Toyota moved development to the market. Honda bought local technology. Stellantis bought a local platform outright and did no development at all.

But they are not equivalent over time. A manufacturer that develops locally can compete in the next cycle on its own terms. One that buys the stack is dependent on suppliers who also serve its competitors. One that licenses the platform has, for that vehicle, become a distributor with a design department — and its cost position is set by a company that competes with it.

The manufacturers that take the third option repeatedly stop being manufacturers. Not immediately, and not visibly for several product cycles, which is precisely the problem.

What this means

The global platform is not dead as engineering. It is dead as strategy. Shared architecture will continue to underpin body-in-white, crash structure and manufacturing tooling, because the economics there are unchanged. What cannot be shared any longer is the part the customer experiences — powertrain calibration, cockpit software, driver assistance, and the cost structure underneath them. Those now diverge by market faster than a global programme can converge.

Local R&D has become a condition of market access rather than an optimisation. Not a legal condition — a competitive one. A manufacturer without local development in China is shipping a product a generation behind on the systems Chinese buyers evaluate first, and no amount of brand equity has proved able to compensate. Chevrolet's Chinese volume fell 92% between 2018 and 2024 while it competed with a substantially combustion line-up.

And the companies that get this right will look, for a while, like they are giving things away. Toyota handing product authority to Chinese engineers and Honda buying its autonomy stack from Momenta both read as retreats. They are the opposite. The retreat is the option that looks like prudence — keeping development at headquarters, shipping a localised variant, and discovering in two cycles that the local competitor now defines the segment.

Indicators

Whether any Western manufacturer establishes genuine Chinese development authority rather than a liaison office. Volkswagen's Chinese engineering arrangements are the test case with the most volume behind them.

Whether Honda's Momenta and CATL arrangements extend beyond China. If Chinese-developed autonomy or battery platforms appear in Honda products for other markets, the localisation has become a global sourcing decision — a much larger shift.

Whether Stellantis repeats the ARGOS structure elsewhere. One licensed platform is a market-specific solution. Two is an operating model.

Whether Chinese European R&D produces Europe-specific product rather than adaptation. Munich offices doing homologation and tuning are one thing. A model conceived in Munich for European buyers would be the same step Honda took in Ohio in the 1970s, and it would arrive with a cost base European manufacturers cannot match.

China's EV Transition Claims Its Biggest Name Yet

Chevrolet sold 641,320 cars in China in 2018 and 52,774 in 2024. It is the largest brand ever withdrawn from the Chinese market — larger than Suzuki and Mitsubishi combined — and the first of GM's three Chinese brands to go, roughly a century after the company's first Shanghai dealership.

On 4 August 2026 General Motors extended its joint venture with SAIC Motor by twenty years, to 2047, and in the same announcement confirmed it will discontinue Chevrolet sales in China. Production continues there, through the separate SAIC-GM-Wuling venture, for export.

The brand being withdrawn is the one GM started with.

A century in China, and how it was built

GM first reached China in the 1920s through a Chevrolet dealership in Shanghai. That business ended in 1949. Negotiations to return began in 1993, and Shanghai GM — a 50:50 joint venture with Shanghai Automotive Industry Corporation — was formed in June 1997.

Buick, not Chevrolet, was the brand GM returned with. The first vehicle off the Shanghai line was a Buick Regal in April 1999, and the reception was such that within a few years the large majority of all Buicks built anywhere were sold in China. A marque that had become an also-ran in the United States was rebuilt as a Chinese premium brand.

The scale that followed was without precedent for a foreign manufacturer. In 2010 Shanghai GM became the first passenger-car maker in China to sell a million vehicles in a single year. GM led all global automakers in the Chinese market for six consecutive years and peaked at 4.04 million units in 2017 — more vehicles than it sold in the United States.

The joint venture that has just been extended to 2047 was signed in 1997. If it runs its term it will be a fifty-year partnership.

What GM has decided

The two halves of the announcement look contradictory and are not.

China remains a factory, for twenty more years. Chevrolet output continues for export. Buick and Cadillac production is directed at the Middle East, Africa, South America, Mexico and other Asian markets. SAIC-GM has committed to at least 30 electric and hybrid models by 2030.

Eight consecutive years of decline is a settled result rather than a slump, so conceding the Chinese consumer was not really a choice. Retaining the cost base is the choice — and on what Chinese plants, supply chains and battery components now cost, it is the defensible one.

What follows

The competition that removed Chevrolet has not stopped. Buick and Cadillac face the same domestic brands and the same electric deficit, and both are already down 67% and 45% from their peaks. The question is not whether Chevrolet was worth withdrawing at 52,774 units, but how long the two GM kept have on the same trajectory.

GM's export platform is also its export competitor. SAIC is one of China's largest vehicle exporters in its own right, principally through MG. GM will now ship Buicks and Cadillacs from plants co-owned by a company shipping its own brands into several of the same destinations.

The destination list is the most informative line in the announcement. The Middle East, Africa, South America, Mexico and other parts of Asia are collectively the major markets that have not imposed origin-based duties on Chinese-built vehicles. The European Union has, and the United States has. Neither is named.

Twenty years is a long commitment to a relationship two governments are actively unwinding. Whatever the logic of keeping the plants, the political exposure is not priced in anything disclosed.

Whether GM discloses China-built export volumes separately. The export arrangement is the substance of the deal and is currently unmeasurable from outside.

In China They Brought the Technology. In India They Are Buying It.

Nikkei reports that global carmakers are replicating their China strategy to gain a foothold in India. The two deals cited run the other way round. In China the foreign manufacturer supplied the engineering and held half the equity; in India it is purchasing the engineering and giving up the equity. That is not the same template — and the template being invoked ended with the foreign partners leaving.

On 12 August 2026, Nikkei Asia reported that global carmakers are turning to Indian partners to establish themselves in India, borrowing from the approach they used in China, with the tie-ups "increasingly crucial to managing capital expenditure." The example given is Honda, which has engaged a Tata Group company on platform development.

The observation that partnerships are multiplying is correct. The comparison to China does not survive contact with what either arrangement actually contains.

What the China strategy was

From 1994, a foreign manufacturer could not build passenger cars in China except through a joint venture in which it held no more than 50%. The requirement was lifted in stages — electric vehicles from the end of 2018, commercial vehicles from 2020, passenger cars from 1 January 2022.

Two features defined it, and both are load-bearing.

It was compulsory. No foreign manufacturer chose a 50:50 structure in China. It was the statutory price of the market.

The foreign partner supplied the technology. The local partner supplied the licence, the land, the distribution and the political standing. The foreign partner supplied the platform, the powertrain, the manufacturing method and the badge. That division is why the knowledge-transfer literature exists at all.

Neither feature applies to India. There has never been a local-ownership requirement for carmakers, which is why Hyundai entered wholly owned in 1996, Kia wholly owned in 2019, and General Motors and Ford operated wholly-owned Indian subsidiaries until they left.

So whatever is happening in India, it is not the thing that happened in China. In India it is voluntary, and when a structure is voluntary the direction of the transaction tells you what is actually scarce.

Take the two live cases.

Honda is buying engineering. Reported on 8 August 2026: Honda has engaged Tata Technologies to develop a new modular platform for petrol, hybrid and electric models across Asia and other markets, excluding North America. Tata Technologies is an engineering services firm, separate from Tata Motors. Honda has historically kept platform development in-house or inside its own supplier network. Here the Indian company is the one holding the capability, and Honda is the customer.

Volkswagen is selling control. Škoda Auto Volkswagen India is reported to be close to a partnership with JSW Group, with JSW taking a majority — 51% is the figure in circulation. Volkswagen had already cut its planned Indian EV platform investment from around $1bn to roughly $700m in November 2025, and had failed to reach a viable programme with Mahindra before that. Its Indian electric cars are now to run on an Indianised version of CMP21, an architecture developed by Volkswagen's Chinese operation in 24 months and reported at 30–40% below the cost of MEB.

That last detail is the only genuine China connection in the story, and it is the opposite of a joint venture. Volkswagen is importing engineering IP that its Chinese business created, into a country where it is simultaneously reducing its own shareholding. Nothing about that resembles Shanghai 1997.

The precedent being copied is not China. It is MG.

The reason this looks like a trend is that one Indian counterparty is doing it twice.

It then worked. MG became the second-largest electric car seller in India behind Tata Motors, largely on one product, the Windsor.

So the model on the table is: a foreign manufacturer contributes the brand and the products, an Indian conglomerate contributes the capital and takes control, and the vehicles are sold under the foreign badge. That is a real and possibly good idea. It has one demonstration, two years of evidence, and one buyer. Two deals by the same acquirer is a counterparty with a thesis, not an industry replicating a playbook.

What is actually being replicated

Something from the Chinese experience is repeating in India. It is in the third and fourth rows of that table, not in the ownership column.

Maruti Suzuki grew 3.54% and fell below 40% share for the first time. Hyundai fell 2.3% and lost more than a point of share. Meanwhile Mahindra grew 19.7% and Tata Motors 14.1%, in a market up 8.39%.

The domestic manufacturers are taking share from the foreign incumbents in a growing market. That is precisely what happened in China from roughly 2018 onward, and it is what removed Chevrolet, Suzuki and Mitsubishi from that market before any of them had a chance to restructure their way out.

The Indian version is much earlier and much gentler — Maruti at 39% is not Chevrolet at 52,774 units, and Toyota grew 18.6% and Kia 13.3%, so foreign is not a category that is losing. But the mechanism is the same one, and it is the part of the China story that a partnership does not address. Selling 51% of your Indian subsidiary to a steel conglomerate does not make the product more competitive against a Scorpio-N.

What the Honda deal actually is

The Honda–Tata Technologies arrangement is the more interesting of the two and is being filed under the wrong heading.

It is not about gaining a foothold in India. The platform is for Asia and other global markets and explicitly excludes North America. Honda sells around 7,500 cars a month in India. The deal is an engineering services contract, placed in India because Indian engineering is capable and cheap, for products largely sold elsewhere.

That is the same movement as Volkswagen having its Chinese operation develop CMP21 and then shipping the architecture to India, and the same movement as Ford restarting its Chennai plant in 2026 to build roughly 235,000 engines a year purely for export. Development and componentry are relocating to wherever they are cheapest to produce, decoupled from where the vehicles are sold. India is winning that on cost and engineering depth.

Calling it a China-style joint venture obscures what is worth noticing: Honda, a company that has never let an outsider design its platforms, has concluded it cannot afford to design them itself. The capital-expenditure pressure in the Nikkei piece is real. The instrument being reached for is not a joint venture; it is outsourcing.

Indicators

Whether a second Indian conglomerate does a JSW-style deal. One buyer doing two transactions is a firm with a strategy. A second buyer, unconnected to JSW, would make it a market.

Whether the Volkswagen–JSW transaction completes, and at what percentage. It is currently reported and unsigned. A minority position for Volkswagen in its own Indian company would be the first case of its kind among serious entrants.

Whether Honda extends the Tata Technologies relationship beyond one platform. One programme is cost control. A second is a decision about where Honda develops cars.

Whether Maruti Suzuki's share stabilises above 35%. The domestic-share argument turns on whether the FY26 move was a step or a trend.

The disconfirming signal. This piece argues that foreign control has been the precondition for foreign success in India, and that the new minority structures therefore carry an unpriced risk. If JSW MG Motor keeps gaining share and the Volkswagen venture reaches meaningful volume under Indian control, the reading is wrong — and the more ordinary explanation, that structure is irrelevant and only the product matters, would be right instead. That explanation is also the one supported by this body of work elsewhere, which is a reason to hold the argument here loosely.

India Taxes Petrol Cars by Size. It Taxes Electric Cars Flat.

The September 2025 GST reform put small petrol cars on 18% and large ones on 40%, and left every electric car on 5% regardless of size. The size test was not extended to electric vehicles, so the incentive to go electric is worth roughly twenty times more on a ₹40 lakh SUV than on a ₹5 lakh hatchback. The one scheme that does condition on price sets a floor, not a ceiling.

India's goods and services tax on cars was restructured with effect from 22 September 2025. Two slabs replaced the old rate-plus-cess structure:

The first two rows are a deliberate size-based progressivity that India has run in some form since the 1980s: small cars are taxed lightly because most buyers can only afford small cars. The third row is a decarbonisation incentive. Each is defensible on its own.

The interaction is not, and it appears to be nobody's decision.

The arithmetic

The electric discount is the gap between what a car would pay on petrol and the flat 5%. That gap is 13 points for a small car and 35 points for a large one — and it applies to a much larger base.

The same policy hands a ₹65,000 inducement to the buyer of a hatchback and a ₹14,00,000 inducement to the buyer of a large SUV. That is a ratio of about 21.5 to 1, and it is entirely a consequence of applying a flat rate on one side of a progressive schedule.

Put the other way: India decided that a petrol car longer than four metres should pay more than twice the tax of one shorter than four metres, and then decided that an electric car should pay 5% whether it is three metres long or five and a half.

The other two levers point the same way

The GST differential is now the main instrument, because the direct one was withdrawn.

Purchase subsidies for electric cars no longer exist. FAME-II ended, and PM E-DRIVE — ₹10,900 crore, covering roughly 25 lakh two-wheelers, 3 lakh three-wheelers and 14,000 buses — excludes electric four-wheelers entirely. The stated reasoning was that the concessional GST rate is sufficient support. That is the rate whose benefit scales with price.

And the import scheme has a price floor. Under SPMEPCI, an approved manufacturer may import up to 8,000 electric cars a year at 15% duty instead of the standing 70–100%, in exchange for ₹4,150 crore of investment and 50% domestic value addition within five years. The vehicles must have a minimum CIF value of US$35,000.

That is the only place in the structure where policy conditions explicitly on price, and it conditions upward. A manufacturer wanting to import an affordable electric car receives nothing; the concession exists only above roughly ₹31 lakh landed, which is a retail price well over ₹40 lakh.

So: no subsidy for cars, a tax break that grows with price, and an import concession available only above a price floor. Three instruments, all tilted the same way, none of which was designed to be.

What the market did anyway

This is the fact that cuts against the argument, and it should come before the argument rather than after it.

Two things follow, and they are not in tension.

The regressive structure has not distorted the volume mix, so far. The bottom of the market is where Indian car demand is, and a ₹65,000 tax advantage on a ₹6 lakh car is a large proportion of that car's price even though it is a small number. Proportionally, the incentive is not regressive at all — it is only regressive in rupees per vehicle.

But rupees per vehicle is what a fiscal incentive costs, and what a buyer at the top receives. BMW's Indian electric volume grew 123.9% to 3,537 units. On the arithmetic above, each of those cars carries a tax concession in the seven figures. The foregone revenue per unit at the top of the market is roughly twenty times that at the bottom, and nothing in the design was intended to produce that.

Why it is likely to matter more from here

The mix is what changes. Mahindra's electric volume rose more than fivefold in FY26 on the BE 6 and XEV 9e, which sit in the ₹18–30 lakh band rather than the ₹10 lakh one. Every manufacturer's electric roadmap moves up-market, because that is where the battery cost is easiest to absorb — and the tax structure rewards exactly that movement.

An incentive that is proportionally neutral and absolutely regressive becomes a fiscal problem when the absolutely-regressive end starts carrying the volume. On current product plans it will.

What a corrected version would look like

Stated as options, not a recommendation: the structure only needs the size test extended across the powertrain boundary.

Apply the existing 4,000mm and ground-clearance tests to electric cars. Small EVs stay at 5%; large EVs move to a second, still-concessional rate — 12% or 18% — preserving the electric advantage everywhere while capping it in rupees. This requires no new definition, only the application of one that already exists.

Or cap the concession in absolute terms — 5% up to a stated value, standard rate above it. This is the more common instrument internationally and the harder one to administer in a market where ex-showroom prices vary by state.

Or leave it, and accept the cost as the price of speed. A defensible position while penetration is 4.3% and the objective is adoption at any price point. It becomes indefensible at 15%.

Indicators

Whether any GST Council paper addresses the electric-vehicle size question. The reform of September 2025 restructured everything else and left this untouched, which suggests it has not been raised rather than that it was considered and rejected.

Whether SPMEPCI's $35,000 floor is revised. It exists to prevent the concession being used for cheap imports, which is a coherent aim; but it is also the clearest statement in Indian policy that the electric benefit is aimed above the mass market.

Whether the ₹20 lakh-plus band's share of electric volume rises materially in FY27. That is the threshold at which the rupee cost of the flat rate becomes visible in revenue terms.

The disconfirming signal. This piece argues the flat rate creates a large and unintended up-market tilt. If electric volume stays concentrated below ₹15 lakh as penetration rises through 10%, the tilt is theoretical — the structure would be generous at the top and simply not used there, which is a much smaller problem than the arithmetic suggests.
